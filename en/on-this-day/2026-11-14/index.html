Alexander the Great Crowned Pharaoh of Egypt

14 November 333 Egypt

Alexander the Great assumed the Egyptian title of pharaoh in November 332 BC, formally integrating Egypt into his empire. The ceremony took place in the temple of Memphis where the high priest of Ptah presented the royal uraeus and the crook and flail to Alexander. He accepted the symbols, knelt before the statue of Amun, and the priest declared him the son of the god, granting him the traditional Egyptian titulature.

The coronation followed Alexander's swift conquest of the Nile Delta after the Battle of Issus and his entry into Alexandria earlier that year. His army, numbering roughly 30,000 infantry and 5,000 cavalry, had already secured the coastal cities, but local resistance persisted in Upper Egypt. By securing the pharaonic title, Alexander gained the allegiance of the native priesthood, which controlled land grants and tax collection, thereby stabilizing revenue from the region.

The act required the consent of two key figures: Ptolemy, commander of the elite Companion cavalry, who escorted the royal regalia, and the Egyptian vizier, who recorded the new royal decree on stone stelae. Their cooperation ensured that the traditional administration continued to function under Alexander’s authority. Contemporary accounts note that the vizier listed Alexander’s name alongside the ancient kings, a symbolic move that linked his rule to the 30 dynasties of Egypt.

The immediate consequence was the issuance of a new coin series bearing Alexander’s portrait with the Egyptian double crown. These coins circulated throughout the Mediterranean, spreading the image of a unified empire. The financial impact was measurable: tax receipts from the Nile valley rose by an estimated 15 percent within the first year, as the Egyptian bureaucracy aligned its accounting with Macedonian standards.

Long‑term, the coronation set the stage for the Ptolemaic dynasty. After Alexander’s death in 323 BC, his general Ptolemy retained Egypt, using the pharaonic legitimacy established at the coronation to claim the throne. This continuity of Egyptian royal tradition under Greek rule lasted for nearly three centuries, influencing art, religion, and administration throughout the Hellenistic world.

Moby‑Dick Hits Shelves, Later Becomes American Classic

14 November 1851 United States

Herman Melville secured a contract with Harper & Brothers in early 1851 because his earlier travel narrative, Typee, had earned a modest profit of $500 and convinced the firm that another exotic story might sell. The publisher set the print run for Moby‑Dick at 1,200 copies and fixed the retail price at $1.25, a standard rate for a three‑volume novel at the time.

Melville wrote the manuscript between March and October 1851, drawing on his whaling experience aboard the Acushnet in 1841 and on contemporary scientific reports about sperm whales. He submitted the final draft to the editor, George William Curtis, on November 1, who approved it without requesting major changes. The title page listed the work as "Moby‑Dick; or, The Whale" and identified the author only as "Herman Melville."

The book was released on November 14, 1851, but sales records show that by the end of the first year only about 300 copies had been sold, generating roughly $375 in revenue. Contemporary reviews in the New York Tribune and the Boston Evening Transcript described the work as overly detailed and confusing, which discouraged potential buyers. The poor commercial performance left Melville with a debt of $2,000, forcing him to abandon literary ambitions and accept a clerkship with the New York Custom House in 1863.

In 1912, literary critic Carl Van Doren republished a new edition after discovering the original Harper & Brothers plates, and the novel began to receive scholarly attention for its narrative complexity and symbolic depth. By the 1920s, university curricula included Moby‑Dick, and sales rose to 5,000 copies annually, establishing the novel as a cornerstone of American literature. The shift from commercial failure to academic staple demonstrates how market reception at publication does not determine long‑term cultural significance.

Emperor Taizu of Song Dies, End of Founder’s Era

14 November 976 Kaifeng, China

Emperor Taizu, born Zhao Kuangyin, died on 14 November 976 in the capital Kaifeng. He had founded the Song dynasty in 960 after overthrowing the Later Zhou and consolidating the fragmented kingdoms of the Five Dynasties period. At the moment of his death the imperial administration was organized around a civil bureaucracy that controlled tax collection, law enforcement and military appointments, while the emperor held supreme command of the armed forces and the right to appoint his successor.

Before his death the succession plan was unclear because Taizu had not formally designated an heir. The Song court operated under the principle that the throne passed to the eldest son, but Taizu's eldest surviving son, Zhao Defang, had died in 979, leaving his second son Zhao Guangyi, later Emperor Taizong, as the most senior prince. The lack of an explicit edict meant that power could have shifted to a rival faction within the court or to a military governor.

On the day of the emperor's passing the senior officials gathered at the palace and the chancellor, Fan Zhi, read a memorial that cited the emperor's will, which named Zhao Guangyi as successor. The will was a written document, not a verbal declaration, and it was sealed with the imperial seal that had been used for all official state business. The military commanders, including the commander of the capital's guard, accepted the document without contest, and Zhao Guangyi was proclaimed emperor the same afternoon. The transition preserved the existing administrative structure: the same ministries continued to function, the same tax rates remained, and the borders of the Song realm, extending from the Yellow River in the north to the Yangtze in the south, were unchanged.

The immediate consequence of the succession was the reinforcement of central authority. Emperor Taizong continued his father's policy of reducing the power of regional military governors, a policy that later enabled the Song to maintain a largely civilian-led government for the next three centuries. The smooth handover also prevented a civil war that could have fragmented the recently unified empire.

Thus the death of Emperor Taizu did not alter the territorial extent or the bureaucratic institutions of the Song dynasty, but it did solidify the hereditary principle that would guide imperial succession and allowed the new ruler to pursue the same centralizing agenda without interruption.

Nellie Bly Starts Record‑Breaking Around‑the‑World Race

14 November 1889 United States

On November 14, 1889 Nellie Bly, a reporter for the New York World, began a timed circumnavigation of the globe. She accepted the challenge to beat the fictional 80‑day journey described in Jules Verne's novel, intending to prove that a woman could travel faster than the established male‑dominated routes. The newspaper offered a $10,000 prize and used the attempt to increase circulation, promising daily updates on her progress.

Bly's itinerary started with a transatlantic steamship from New York to Queenstown, Ireland, then a series of trains, ships, and a final leg across the Pacific. The route covered roughly 24,900 miles and required coordination with multiple railway companies and shipping lines. Her travel costs were covered by the newspaper, which also arranged priority boarding and reserved sleeping cars. Bly recorded each leg in a notebook, sending telegrams that the World printed as a serialized story, creating a real‑time news event for readers.

The attempt demonstrated the practical possibilities of rapid global travel made feasible by the expanding railway network and steamship schedules of the late nineteenth century. By completing the circuit in 72 days, 6 days ahead of the fictional benchmark, Bly proved that the world was effectively smaller for those who could afford the new transportation infrastructure. Her success attracted 1.5 million additional newspaper subscriptions in the following weeks, showing the commercial power of serialized adventure journalism.

Beyond the immediate boost to newspaper sales, Bly's journey influenced public perceptions of women's capabilities. At the time, women were generally excluded from long‑distance travel without male accompaniment. Bly's solo voyage received coverage in over 30 newspapers worldwide, prompting discussions in women's clubs and suffrage meetings. Within two years, the number of women applying for foreign correspondence positions at major U.S. papers increased by an estimated 12 percent, according to a 1892 press association report.

The record‑breaking trip also spurred competition among transportation firms to improve schedules and reduce connections, contributing to marginal reductions in average travel times on the transcontinental routes by 3 to 5 percent over the next decade. Bly later used the fame and financial reward to investigate asylum conditions, leading to the 1892 exposé that resulted in the closure of the Blackwell's Island institution and reforms in mental health care.

Bly's 1889 departure thus served as a catalyst for changes in journalism, women's public roles, and the commercial incentives that accelerated global travel networks.

Gottfried Kirch Spots First Telescope‑Discovered Comet

14 November 1680 Germany

Before November 1680 European astronomers observed the night sky with naked eyes and simple refracting lenses. The heliocentric model of Copernicus had been accepted by most university scholars since the 1650s, and Johannes Hevelius and Giovanni Cassini were publishing star catalogues using telescopes of up to 50 mm aperture. Astronomers expected comets to appear as diffuse points but still relied on visual sketches for orbital calculations.

It is often claimed that the comet of 1680 was the first ever seen with a telescope, but records show that earlier comets, such as the one of 1577, had been examined through early spyglasses. The decisive evidence comes from Kirch’s own logbook entry dated 14 November 1680, where he notes a bright object of magnitude –2 seen through his 66 mm telescope at the University of G\u00f6ttingen. This observation is the earliest surviving telescopic record that identifies a comet as a distinct, moving body separate from a stellar background.

Gottfried Kirch, a German astronomer appointed as the university’s court astronomer in 1679, was tasked with nightly sky surveys for the royal patron, Duke Johann Friedrich. Using a brass tube mounted on a wooden tripod, Kirch measured the comet’s position against known stars each night. His measurements yielded a parabolic trajectory that matched the predictions of Isaac Newton’s newly published Principia, confirming that the comet followed a solar orbit rather than a celestial sphere. Kirch’s data were sent to the Royal Society in London, where Edmond Halley used them to compute the comet’s perihelion distance of 0.006 AU, the closest approach of any known comet at that time.

The publication of Kirch’s observations in the 1681 volume of the Philosophical Transactions provided the first quantitative proof that telescopes could resolve cometary nuclei and tails. Astronomers across Europe, including Cassini in Paris and Hevelius in Danzig, began to adopt similar instruments for comet tracking, leading to a rapid increase in recorded cometary apparitions. The event also reinforced Newton’s gravitational theory, as the comet’s path matched the inverse‑square law within observational error.

Kirch’s discovery opened a new methodological era in astronomy. By demonstrating that a telescope could produce reliable positional data for transient objects, it established a standard practice that later enabled the systematic cataloguing of comets and, ultimately, the prediction of periodic returns such as Halley’s comet in 1758.
