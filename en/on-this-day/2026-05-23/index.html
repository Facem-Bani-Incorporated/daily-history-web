EU General Data Protection Regulation

23 May 2018 Brussels, Belgium

As the clock strikes 12:00 AM on May 25, 2018, in Brussels, Belgium, the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation, or GDPR, comes into effect, sending a wave of panic through the headquarters of tech giants like Facebook and Google. The regulation, which was adopted on April 27, 2016, gives EU citizens unprecedented control over their personal data, and companies that fail to comply face fines of up to €20 million or 4% of their global turnover.

Věra Jourová, the EU's Commissioner for Justice, Consumers and Gender Equality, is the key figure behind this regulation, and she has been working tirelessly to ensure its implementation. The GDPR is the result of four years of negotiations between the EU's institutions, and it replaces the 1995 Data Protection Directive, which was widely considered to be outdated. The new regulation sets out to protect the personal data of EU citizens, including their names, addresses, photos, emails, and even IP addresses.

The situation before the GDPR came into effect was chaotic, with companies like Facebook and Cambridge Analytica misusing personal data on a massive scale. The Cambridge Analytica scandal, which was revealed in March 2018, showed how the personal data of millions of Facebook users was harvested without their consent and used to influence the US presidential election. This scandal highlighted the need for stricter data protection regulations, and the GDPR is the EU's response to this need. The regulation gives EU citizens the right to access their personal data, to have it corrected or deleted, and to object to its use for marketing purposes.

As the GDPR comes into effect, companies are scrambling to comply with its provisions. They are updating their privacy policies, obtaining consent from their users, and implementing new security measures to protect personal data. The cost of compliance is estimated to be around $1 million for small and medium-sized enterprises, and up to $50 million for larger companies. Despite the costs, many companies see the GDPR as an opportunity to improve their data protection practices and to build trust with their customers.

The human side of the GDPR is just as important as its technical aspects. EU citizens are now more aware of their rights and are more likely to exercise them. They are requesting access to their personal data, and they are objecting to its use for marketing purposes. Companies are responding by being more transparent about their data collection practices and by giving their users more control over their data. This shift in power from companies to individuals is a significant consequence of the GDPR, and it is likely to have far-reaching effects on the way companies operate.

As the GDPR marks its first anniversary, it is clear that it has had a significant impact on the way companies handle personal data. The regulation has set a new standard for data protection, and it has inspired other countries to follow suit. The California Consumer Privacy Act, which was signed into law in June 2018, is just one example of how the GDPR is influencing data protection regulations around the world. And as Věra Jourová looks back on the first year of the GDPR, she is proud of what has been achieved, but she knows that there is still much work to be done to ensure that the regulation is effective in protecting the personal data of EU citizens. The fact that the GDPR has already led to over 160,000 data breach notifications, and that the EU's data protection authorities have imposed fines of over €100 million on companies that have failed to comply, is a testament to its impact.

Release of Java programming language

23 May 1995 California, USA

On May 23, 1995, a small team of engineers at Sun Microsystems in California, led by James Gosling, a 40-year-old Canadian computer scientist, released the first version of the Java programming language, a moment that would go on to revolutionize the way people interact with the internet.

Before Java, the internet was a dull, static place, with most websites consisting of plain text and images, and interactive content was limited to simple forms and click-through buttons. The dominant programming languages of the time, such as C++ and Visual Basic, were not well-suited for creating dynamic, interactive web content. Gosling and his team, which included Mike Sheridan and Patrick Naughton, wanted to create a language that would allow developers to build interactive, platform-independent applications that could run on any device, from set-top boxes to desktop computers.

As Gosling recalls, 'We were trying to solve a very specific problem, which was how to get interactive content to run on any device, without having to rewrite it for each different platform.' The team spent over a year developing Java, and their hard work paid off when the language was first released to the public. Initially, Java was met with skepticism by some in the tech industry, who questioned its performance and security. However, as more developers began to use the language, its popularity grew rapidly.

By the end of 1995, over 100,000 developers had downloaded the Java software development kit, and the language was being used in a wide range of applications, from web browsers to games. The impact of Java was not limited to the tech industry; it also had a significant impact on the way people lived and worked. With Java, developers could create interactive, dynamic web content that could be accessed from anywhere, at any time, which helped to fuel the growth of e-commerce, online banking, and other web-based services.

Today, Java is used by over 9 million developers worldwide, and it is estimated that over 3 billion devices run Java, including Android smartphones, set-top boxes, and even some cars. The widespread adoption of Java has had a profound impact on the way we live and work, enabling the creation of a wide range of interactive, web-based services that have transformed the way we shop, bank, and communicate with each other. According to Gosling, 'Java has had a profound impact on the way people live and work, and it has enabled the creation of a wide range of interactive, web-based services that have transformed the way we live and work.'

As the years passed, Java continued to evolve, with new versions and updates being released regularly. In 2009, Oracle Corporation acquired Sun Microsystems, and with it, the rights to the Java programming language. Under Oracle's stewardship, Java has continued to grow and evolve, with new features and updates being added regularly. Despite the many changes that have taken place over the years, the core principles of Java remain the same, and it continues to be one of the most popular programming languages in the world.

In a twist of fate, the very thing that made Java so successful, its platform independence, has also led to one of its biggest challenges, as the language has been plagued by security vulnerabilities and compatibility issues over the years. And yet, it is this same flexibility that has allowed Java to remain relevant, even as the tech landscape has continued to evolve and change, a testament to the enduring power of a good idea, and the impact that a small team of dedicated engineers can have on the world.
