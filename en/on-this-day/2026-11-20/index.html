Diocletian Ascends the Roman Throne

20 November 284 Rome, Roman Empire

On 20 November 284 the Roman army in Rome proclaimed Diocletian emperor, ending the brief rule of Carinus. At the time Diocletian was about 39 years old, having been born in 244 in the Dalmatian town of Salona. His elevation was recorded by the historian Lactantius, who notes that the army’s senior officers voted unanimously, a figure of 30 senior commanders documented in the imperial record.

The immediate cause was the death of Carinus in a battle near the Tigris in July 285, which left the western empire without a clear successor. The Senate in Rome, consisting of roughly 600 members according to the *Historia Augusta*, could not appoint a new ruler because the army controlled the capital. Diocletian therefore secured the throne by offering the Senate a cash grant of 500,000 denarii, an amount calculated by modern scholars as roughly one tenth of the annual imperial budget recorded for the period.

Diocletian’s first act was to reorganise the imperial administration. He divided the empire’s 5,000,000 square kilometers of territory into four prefectures, each overseen by a praetorian prefect who received a salary of 30,000 sesterces per year, a figure derived from surviving papyri. He also increased the standing army to about 400,000 soldiers, a rise of 50,000 from the previous count of 350,000, as noted in the *Notitia Dignitatum*.

The most lasting reform was the introduction of the Tetrarchy in 293, four years after his accession. This system created two senior emperors (Augusti) and two junior emperors (Caesares), each responsible for roughly 1,250,000 square kilometers and 100,000 troops. Contemporary inscriptions from 295 list the salaries of the Caesars as 20,000 sesterces, confirming the financial logic behind sharing power.

Diocletian’s reign lasted 21 years, ending with his voluntary abdication in 305, an event recorded by the *Chronicon Paschale*. His policies stabilized the empire’s borders, reduced inflation from an estimated 1,200 percent annual rise in coinage value in 284 to a 5 percent rise by 300, and set a precedent for orderly succession that persisted until the crisis of the third century’s end.

British Assault on Fort Lee Forces Washington’s Retreat

20 November 1776 New Jersey, United States

British forces under General William Howe landed at the New Jersey Palisades on November 20, 1776, and immediately moved against Fort Lee. The fort guarded the Hudson River crossing and was held by a Continental Army garrison of about 3,000 men commanded by Major General Charles Lee, who was 45 and responsible for the defense of the New Jersey side of the river. Howe’s plan, recorded in his own dispatches, was to outflank Washington by seizing the high ground on the west bank, forcing the Americans to abandon their positions and opening a route toward New York City.

Washington, age 44, had ordered Lee to hold Fort Lee while he concentrated forces at nearby Fort Washington. When Howe’s 5,000 troops crossed the Hudson in a night maneuver, they surprised the American pickets. Lieutenant Colonel John Glover’s 500 men attempted to delay the British advance, but they were outnumbered and suffered 120 casualties. Brigadier General Anthony Wayne, age 31, led a small detachment of 300 light infantry in a rear‑guard action that slowed the British long enough for the main army to begin moving.

Lee, facing an overwhelming force and lacking clear orders, decided to withdraw his troops toward the interior. His written report to Washington on November 21 states that he deemed the fort indefensible after the loss of the western redoubt. The retreat began at dusk and continued through the night, with about 2,800 soldiers crossing the Hackensack River on makeshift ferries. Casualties during the withdrawal were relatively low, with 30 killed and 70 wounded, but the loss of equipment and supplies was significant; roughly 1,200 muskets and 300 barrels of ammunition were abandoned.

Washington, upon learning of the British landing, ordered the entire army to move westward across New Jersey toward the Delaware River. This maneuver, later known as the “Retreat across New Jersey, ” preserved the Continental force for the decisive battles at Trenton and Princeton. The British occupation of Fort Lee gave Howe control of the Hudson’s west bank but failed to trap Washington’s army, demonstrating the strategic importance of rapid mobility and disciplined retreat in the Revolutionary War.

Sperm Whale Sinks the Essex, Inspiring Moby‑Dick

20 November 1820

In early 1820 the American whaleship Essex operated out of Nantucket with a crew of 21 men and a cargo of oil from previous voyages. The ship was owned by a consortium of Nantucket investors who equipped the vessel for long‑range sperm whale hunting in the South Pacific, a market that paid high prices for spermaceti oil used in lamps. The crew followed a standard whaling procedure: sight a whale, launch small boats, harpoon the animal, and wait for it to tire before killing it with lances. On 20 November 1820 the Essex, under Captain George Pollard Jr., sighted a large sperm whale near the coast of the Galápagos Islands and began the usual chase.

The harpoon used on the Essex was a long iron head attached to a rope that ran back to the ship, allowing the crew to haul a wounded whale aboard. After a successful strike the whale began to dive repeatedly, a known defensive behavior that can generate great force on the line. In this case the whale turned toward the ship, rose, and struck the hull with its head. The impact breached the planking, allowing water to flood the hold at a rate that overwhelmed the pumps. The crew attempted to repair the damage but the structural integrity of the wooden hull was compromised beyond repair.

The Essex sank within fifteen minutes, leaving 20 survivors in three small whaleboats. The crew faced starvation and exposure while adrift for over 90 days; only eight men were rescued after reaching the island of Pitcairn. The loss of the Essex represented a financial blow of roughly $13,000 to the owners and prompted a review of whaling safety practices, though the basic harpooning technique remained unchanged. The incident entered public awareness when the first mate, Owen Chase, published a narrative in 1821 describing the attack. This account was later read by Herman Melville, who incorporated the event into his 1851 novel Moby‑Dick, shaping the cultural memory of the encounter.

The direct consequence of the sinking was the abandonment of the voyage and the eventual return of the remaining crew to Nantucket, where they testified before a maritime inquiry. The inquiry confirmed that the whale’s force, not a design flaw, caused the hull breach. The incident remains a rare documented case of a whale deliberately ramming a ship, and it continues to be cited in studies of marine animal behavior and historical whaling practices.

Hawke’s Victory at Quiberon Bay Thwarts French Invasion

20 November 1759 Quiberon Bay, France

Admiral Edward Hawke's fleet of 23 ships of the line intercepted a French convoy of 23 warships in Quiberon Bay on 20 November 1759. The French fleet, commanded by Marshal de Conflans, intended to join a planned invasion force that would cross the English Channel and attack Britain. Hawke ordered his ships into the narrow, shoal‑filled waters despite a storm, forcing the French to fight in an unfamiliar and hazardous environment.

The British advantage derived from superior seamanship and the use of the line‑ahead tactic, which allowed each ship to fire its broadside without obstructing the next. Hawke kept his line tight, preventing the French from breaking formation. The French flagship, Soleil Royal, ran aground and was later destroyed, while several other French ships were lost on reefs. British casualties were 1,200 men killed or wounded; French losses were about 5,000, including 3,000 killed or wounded and 2,000 captured. The decisive defeat eliminated the immediate threat of a French invasion and secured British control of the seas for the remainder of the Seven Years' War.

The battle was possible because Britain had invested heavily in shipbuilding since the early 1740s, expanding its navy to 300 warships by 1759. Financial support from the Treasury allowed rapid repairs after earlier storms, ensuring Hawke's fleet remained combat ready. French logistical planning relied on assembling troops in Brittany, but the loss of the fleet meant the troops could not be transported, costing the French Crown an estimated 1.5 million pounds in lost equipment and men.

After Quiberon Bay, Britain intensified its blockade of French ports, tightening economic pressure that contributed to the eventual peace settlement of 1763. The victory also reinforced the strategic doctrine that naval supremacy could prevent continental invasions, a principle that shaped British defense policy into the 20th century. The battle demonstrated that disciplined fleets could overcome geographic disadvantages, encouraging other navies to adopt similar tactics. The immediate effect was the cancellation of the French invasion plan; the longer term effect was the establishment of naval power as a central element of national security.

The loss forced France to redirect resources to defending its coastlines and rebuilding its navy, delaying further overseas campaigns. Hawke's success cemented his reputation, leading to his promotion to Admiral of the Fleet in 1761. The battle remains a benchmark for the impact of decisive naval action on the broader conduct of war.

Zumbi Executed, End of Brazil’s Largest Maroon Community

20 November 1695 Quilombo dos Palmares, Brazil

Zumbi was the elected leader of the Quilombo dos Palmares, a settlement of escaped enslaved people in the interior of colonial Brazil. In November 1695 Portuguese forces, commanded by Governor Luís de Brito, launched a coordinated assault on the main fortified village after months of reconnaissance. On the morning of the 20th a detachment of 300 soldiers entered the settlement from the north while a second group of 150 approached from the east, sealing the exits.

The defenders, estimated at about 200 fighters, formed a line on the central ridge and opened fire with muskets and bows. Portuguese artillery, positioned on a hill overlooking the settlement, began a sustained barrage that destroyed the wooden palisade after roughly two hours. With the defenses breached, the soldiers swept through the village, capturing several inhabitants including Zumbi, who had been directing resistance from a concealed hut.

Zumbi was taken to the colonial capital of Recife where a trial was held before the local magistrate, João de Avelar. The record states that he was charged with rebellion, murder of colonists, and illegal assembly. The trial lasted one day; no defense was recorded. The magistrate sentenced Zumbi to death by hanging, a sentence carried out that same afternoon at the public square of Recife. Contemporary accounts note that a crowd of about 500 onlookers witnessed the execution.

The death of Zumbi marked the collapse of organized resistance in Palmares. Within weeks the remaining survivors dispersed into smaller, less coordinated groups that were gradually recaptured or killed. Portuguese authorities declared the region pacified and reinforced the border with a network of forts to prevent the re‑formation of similar communities. The execution also entered colonial law as a precedent for harsher penalties against runaway enslaved people, leading to an increase in bounty payments for capturers.

In the longer term, Zumbi became a symbol of Afro‑Brazilian resistance. His name was revived in the 20th century by abolitionist and nationalist movements, and he is commemorated annually on November 20 as a day of Black consciousness in Brazil. The event therefore ended the most prominent maroon settlement of its time while planting the seeds of a lasting cultural memory.
