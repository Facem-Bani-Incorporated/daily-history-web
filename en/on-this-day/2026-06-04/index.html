Dunkirk evacuation ends

4 June 1940 Dunkirk, France

As the last Allied soldier steps off the shore of Dunkirk, France, and onto a waiting ship, the sheer scale of the evacuation becomes clear: over 338,000 men rescued from the jaws of the German army, a feat that will be remembered for generations to come. But what most people don't know is that this miraculous rescue was made possible by a combination of bad weather, a hesitant German commander, and a small group of brave sailors who risked everything to save their comrades.

The situation before the evacuation was dire: the German army had pushed the Allies back to the sea, and it seemed inevitable that they would be captured or killed. The British Expeditionary Force, led by General Lord Gort, had been fighting in France for months, but they were vastly outnumbered and outgunned. As the Germans closed in, the British government realized that their only hope was to evacuate as many soldiers as possible from the beaches of Dunkirk.

The evacuation itself was a complex and chaotic operation, involving hundreds of ships and boats of all sizes, from large naval vessels to small civilian yachts. The Royal Navy, led by Vice-Admiral Bertram Ramsay, played a crucial role in organizing the evacuation, but it was the small boats that did much of the heavy lifting, ferrying soldiers from the beach to the larger ships waiting offshore. As the soldiers waited on the beach, they were under constant attack from German aircraft, and many were killed or wounded before they could even get on a boat.

One of the key figures in the evacuation was a man named Charles Lightoller, a retired naval officer who had commanded a ship during World War I. At the age of 66, Lightoller sailed his own yacht, the Sundowner, to Dunkirk, where he helped to evacuate over 130 soldiers. As he later recalled, 'We were under fire the whole time, but we didn't have much trouble getting the men on board.' The Sundowner made two trips to Dunkirk, rescuing a total of 130 soldiers, and Lightoller was later awarded the Distinguished Service Cross for his bravery.

As the evacuation drew to a close, the numbers were staggering: over 338,000 soldiers rescued, including more than 198,000 British and over 140,000 French, Belgian, and Dutch troops. The cost had been high, however: over 68,000 Allied soldiers had been killed or wounded during the Battle of France, and many more had been left behind in the chaos of the evacuation.

The irony is that the Dunkirk evacuation, which is often remembered as a great British victory, was actually a defeat: the Allies had been forced to retreat from France, leaving behind much of their equipment and many of their comrades. As Winston Churchill, the British Prime Minister, later said, 'Wars are not won by evacuations.' And yet, the Dunkirk evacuation remains one of the most famous and inspiring stories of World War II, a testament to the bravery and ingenuity of the soldiers and sailors who took part in it.

Battle of Midway begins

4 June 1942 Midway Atoll, Pacific Ocean

As the sun rises over the Midway Atoll on June 4, 1942, the sound of waves gently lapping against the shore is shattered by the roar of Japanese aircraft, marking the beginning of the Battle of Midway, a pivotal moment in the Pacific War. The atoll, a tiny speck of land in the vast Pacific Ocean, is home to a strategic US naval base, and the Japanese aim to capture it, drawing out the US Pacific Fleet and destroying it.

The situation before the battle is tense, with the Japanese having already launched a surprise attack on Pearl Harbor just six months prior, killing 2,403 Americans and destroying much of the US Pacific Fleet. The US is still reeling from the attack, but the Japanese are determined to deliver another crushing blow. Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, the commander of the Japanese Combined Fleet, has devised a complex plan to lure the US fleet into a trap, using the Midway Atoll as bait.

The battle is fierce, with Japanese aircraft swarming the skies and US planes scrambled to defend the atoll. The US has broken Japanese naval codes, allowing them to anticipate the attack and prepare an ambush. As the Japanese aircraft approach, US planes are waiting, and the skies erupt in a frenzy of gunfire and explosions. The Japanese lose four aircraft carriers, the Akagi, Kaga, Soryu, and Hiryu, as well as one heavy cruiser and hundreds of experienced pilots, a blow from which they will never recover.

The human side of the battle is just as compelling, with brave pilots on both sides risking everything to gain the upper hand. Lieutenant Commander Clarence McClusky, a US Navy pilot, leads a group of dive bombers in a daring attack on the Japanese carriers, scoring direct hits and sinking two of them. The Japanese, meanwhile, are fighting for their lives, with pilots like Lieutenant Tomonaga, who leads a group of torpedo bombers in a desperate bid to sink the US carriers.

In the aftermath of the battle, the consequences are staggering. The Japanese have lost 3,057 men, while the US has lost 362. The Japanese navy will never recover from the loss of its experienced pilots and aircraft carriers, and the US gains the initiative in the Pacific. The battle marks a turning point in the war, as the US begins to push back against Japanese expansion. Over the next three years, the US will island-hop across the Pacific, slowly but surely closing in on the Japanese mainland.

The ripple effects of the battle will be felt for decades to come. The US will emerge from the war as a dominant world power, while the Japanese will be forced to rebuild and re-evaluate their place in the world. The battle will also have a profound impact on the development of naval warfare, as the importance of aircraft carriers and air power becomes clear. And yet, despite its significance, the Battle of Midway remains a relatively forgotten moment in history, overshadowed by more dramatic events like the D-Day invasion of Normandy.

The one detail that reframes the story is that the Japanese were so confident of victory that they had already begun to make plans for the occupation of the Midway Atoll, including the issuance of special currency and the establishment of a military government. As the battle rages on, these plans are rendered obsolete, a testament to the unpredictability of war and the bravery of those who fought in it.
