Darwin Publishes 'On the Origin of Species'

24 November 1859 London, United Kingdom

Charles Darwin, a 40‑year‑old naturalist, released the book that would reshape biology. On 24 November 1859 the volume titled On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection was printed by John Murray in London and made available to the public.

The work argued that species change over time because individuals with traits better suited to their environment produce more offspring. Darwin presented evidence from his five‑year voyage on HMS Beagle, including 350 specimens of finches from the Galápagos Islands, and from breeding experiments with pigeons that showed inherited variation. He also cited the fossil record, noting that older strata contain forms increasingly different from those in younger layers. The central mechanism, natural selection, was explained as the differential survival of those variations.

John Murray, the publisher, agreed to issue the book after Darwin sent a manuscript of 350 pages in early 1859. Murray set the price at £5, a sum that limited initial sales to educated readers; the first edition sold 1,250 copies. The book’s frontispiece featured a diagram of a tree of life, designed by Darwin’s friend the geologist Sir Charles Lyell, who had previously championed uniformitarian geology and encouraged Darwin to publish. Lyell wrote a supportive preface, stating that the work offered a “new principle” for explaining the diversity of life.

Among the reviewers, the botanist Joseph Dalton Hooker, a close confidant of Darwin, provided a detailed critique in The Times, affirming the evidence and helping to legitise the theory among scientists. In contrast, the Anglican bishop Samuel Wilberforce delivered a famous debate in 1860, questioning the plausibility of gradual change, which highlighted the controversy among clergy and the educated public.

The immediate consequence was a rapid spread of the idea through scientific societies, with 24 new editions issued by 1872. Universities began to include evolutionary theory in curricula, and the Royal Society elected Darwin as a fellow in 1839, a status that facilitated acceptance. However, the publication also provoked opposition from religious institutions, leading to public disputes that persisted into the 20th century. The book laid the groundwork for modern genetics, which later confirmed the mechanism of inheritance that Darwin could not explain.

The lasting effect of the 1859 publication is the establishment of evolution as the unifying framework of biology, guiding research from comparative anatomy to molecular biology.

Genghis Khan Wins Battle of the Indus, Completes Central Asian Conquest

24 November 1221 Indus River region (present-day Pakistan)

In the spring of 1221 Genghis Khan had already overrun the Khwarazmian Empire from the east, capturing Bukhara and Samarkand and forcing the Shah to flee. The Shah’s son, Jalal al‑Din, gathered a force of roughly 30,000 men, including cavalry from the western provinces, and moved westward to avoid total destruction. By November the remnants of the Khwarazmian army had crossed the Indus River near modern Multan, seeking the river as a defensive barrier.

Genghis ordered his main force, estimated at 100,000 mounted archers, to pursue across the desert steppe. The Mongol strategy relied on rapid movement, feigned retreats and encirclement. At the Indus crossing Jalal al‑Din positioned his troops on the northern bank, hoping the river would hinder the Mongol cavalry. The Mongols forced a crossing at a shallow ford, then divided into three wings. One wing circled to the east to cut off any retreat, another pressed directly against the Khwarazmian line, and the third held reserve behind a sand dune.

The Khwarazmian front collapsed after a volley of arrows broke their shield wall; Mongol horsemen then charged, killing or routing most of the infantry. Contemporary Persian chroniclers record that of the 30,000 defenders, about 12,000 were killed and the rest fled downstream. Jalal al‑Din escaped with a small escort, crossing the river on a boat and later seeking refuge in the Delhi Sultanate.

The defeat eliminated organized resistance in Central Asia. Within weeks the Mongols occupied the remaining towns of the Khwarazmian heartland, and by early 1222 they had secured the trade routes from Samarkand to the Persian Gulf. The loss also forced the Delhi Sultanate to confront a potential Mongol threat, shaping its military reforms for the following decades. The battle therefore marked the final phase of the Mongol conquest of Central Asia and set the stage for further westward expansion toward the Caucasus and Eastern Europe.

Fourth Crusade Sacks Christian Zara to Pay Venetian Debt

24 November 1221 Zara (now Zadar, Croatia)

The Fourth Crusade army arrived at the Adriatic port of Zara and forced its surrender to satisfy a debt owed to the Republic of Venice. Prior to the siege, Zara was an autonomous city‑state under the protection of the Holy Roman Empire, with its own council, walls, and a population estimated at 20,000. The crusaders, organized by Pope Innocent III, had been promised transport by Venice in exchange for a payment of 85,000 silver marks, a sum that the leaders could not raise.

Venetian merchants supplied a fleet of about 85 ships and demanded the full amount before departure. When the crusaders could pay only a fraction, the Venetians warned that the expedition would be delayed. To avoid losing the fleet, the crusader leaders, including Count Baldwin of Flanders and Doge Enrico Dandolo, agreed to capture a wealthy city that would provide the needed funds. Zara, a rival of Venice and a Christian city, was chosen because its harbor could accommodate the fleet and its treasury was known to contain substantial tax revenues.

On November 24, the crusader force of roughly 30,000 men, including knights, infantry, and sailors, surrounded the city walls. The defenders, numbering about 5,000, attempted to repel the attackers but lacked comparable siege equipment. After a brief artillery bombardment with mangonels, the crusaders breached the western gate and entered the market district. Contemporary chronicles record that 2,000 civilians were killed and 5,000 were taken prisoner. The remaining population was forced to pay a tribute of 85,000 marks, which was transferred to the Venetian treasury.

The immediate result was the loss of Zara’s independence; the city was placed under Venetian control and its council was replaced by a pro‑Venetian administration. This transaction allowed the crusade to continue toward Constantinople, where the same financial pressures later led to the infamous sack of the Byzantine capital. The capture of Zara demonstrated how monetary obligations could override religious objectives, and it set a precedent for the use of crusader forces as a tool of Venetian commercial expansion.

Abel Tasman First European to Spot Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania)

24 November 1642 Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania, Australia)

Abel Tasman, a captain of the Dutch East India Company, sighted the island now called Tasmania on 24 November 1642. He was on a commission to find new trade routes and possible sources of valuable spices, a goal that drove the Company to fund long voyages into the largely unknown southern seas.

Tasman commanded two ships, the Heemskerck and the Zeehaen, each carrying about 150 men and a cargo of trade goods, charts, and scientific instruments. After rounding the southern tip of New Holland, they entered a strait that would later bear Tasman's name. The crew recorded the coastline, noting its length of roughly 300 kilometres and the presence of dense forests and abundant wildlife. Their charts marked the island as Van Diemen’s Land, after the Dutch governor of the East Indies, and they claimed it for the United Provinces of the Netherlands.

The immediate consequence was the addition of a sizeable landmass to European maps, expanding the known world by an area of about 68,000 square kilometres. This new entry prompted other Dutch and later British explorers to revisit the region, although permanent European settlement did not begin until the early nineteenth century. The Dutch never established a colony there, but the charts they produced were used by later navigators, including James Cook, whose 1770 voyage confirmed the island's existence and its separation from mainland Australia.

For the Indigenous peoples of Tasmania, the Dutch sighting marked the first recorded contact by Europeans, though no direct encounter occurred. Their societies continued unchanged for another 150 years until British settlement introduced disease, dispossession, and violent conflict, ultimately reducing the Aboriginal population from an estimated 5,000‑8,000 to a handful of survivors by the mid‑nineteenth century. The name Van Diemen’s Land persisted in European literature until the island was renamed Tasmania in 1856 in honor of Tasman himself, a rare case where a European explorer’s name replaced the original colonial label.

The lasting impact of Tasman's 1642 voyage lies in the cartographic legacy that opened the southern Pacific to systematic exploration and trade planning. The accurate charts enabled later navigators to avoid hazardous reefs and to plan resupply points, which facilitated the expansion of European maritime commerce across the Indian and Pacific Oceans. The island’s later colonisation and the tragic consequences for its Aboriginal inhabitants are direct outcomes of the initial European claim made by Tasman’s expedition.

Scott Joplin, the 'King of Ragtime,' Is Born

24 November 1868 Texarkana, Texas, United States

Scott Joplin was born on November 24, 1868, in Texarkana, Texas. His parents were African‑American former slaves; his father, Giles Joplin, worked as a laborer and his mother, Lucy Glover, was a domestic worker. The birth occurred at a time when African‑American musicians were limited to church music, folk songs, and minstrel shows, and there was no recognized market for solo piano composition among black performers.

Joplin learned piano basics from a local teacher, but his formal education ended after the fourth grade. In the 1880s he moved to Sedalia, Missouri, where a thriving ragtime scene existed in local clubs and dance halls. Ragtime combined syncopated African rhythmic patterns with the harmonic structure of European march music. Joplin refined this hybrid by writing sheet music that preserved the syncopation while providing clear notation for pianists. His first published piece, "Original Rags, " appeared in 1895 and sold 25,000 copies, a figure unprecedented for a black composer at that time.

The mechanism that made Joplin’s work transformative was the standardization of ragtime notation. By encoding syncopation in a written form, he enabled pianists nationwide to reproduce the style without oral transmission. This allowed ragtime to spread from regional dance venues to middle‑class parlors and eventually to the emerging recording industry. Joplin’s most famous composition, "Maple Leaf Rag, " sold over one million copies of sheet music by 1910 and was recorded by early phonograph companies, cementing ragtime as a commercial genre.

Joplin’s success also altered the cultural economy for African‑American musicians. Prior to his career, publishing houses rarely accepted works by black composers; after his breakthrough, at least twelve black composers secured contracts with major firms between 1900 and 1915. However, the rise of jazz in the 1920s reduced ragtime’s popularity, and Joplin died in poverty on April 1, 1917, in New York City. Decades later, a 1970s revival of his music, sparked by the film "The Sting, " re‑introduced his compositions to new audiences and led to his posthumous Pulitzer Prize in 1976.

The lasting consequence of Joplin’s birth and career is that ragtime established a precedent for African‑American composers to write, publish, and profit from complex instrumental music. This precedent paved the way for later jazz, blues, and popular music forms that dominate global soundscapes today.
