USS Indianapolis Sunk by Japanese Torpedoes

18 July 1945 Philippine Sea

At 2:14 a.m. on July 30, 1945, the USS Indianapolis was struck by the first of two Japanese torpedoes. The ship's commanding officer, Captain Charles McVay III, was in his cabin asleep. He woke to the impact—a violent upward lurch that threw him from his bunk. The first torpedo had pierced the starboard side, just forward of the midship section. The second followed twelve minutes later, hitting almost the same spot. By then, the crew already knew they were sinking.

The Indianapolis was a heavy cruiser, 610 feet long, built to survive what the ocean could throw at it. Except this. The torpedoes came from the Japanese submarine I-58, commanded by Commander Hashimoto Moshiemon, who had been waiting in the Philippine Sea for exactly this kind of target. The ship had just completed a secret mission—delivering uranium and bomb components for what would become the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima—and was en route to Guam, running without escort, without zigzagging, without sonar. It was a decision made in confidence. The war in the Pacific was nearly finished. Risk seemed calculated and acceptable.

The flooding was catastrophic and immediate. The second torpedo had ruptured the fuel tanks and the forward engine room. Within twelve minutes, the Indianapolis began her final dive. Captain McVay gave the order to abandon ship at 2:35 a.m. By 2:52 a.m., she was gone—slipped beneath the surface in darkness, taking 300 men with her.

But the real horror was only beginning. Of the 1,196 crew members who made it into the water, 879 found themselves floating in the Philippine Sea with no lifeboats, no life jackets for many, no rescue on the way. The Navy didn't know they were down. No distress signal had been sent. No one was looking for them.

For four days, the men drifted. Sharks came. Dehydration set in. Hallucinations followed. Men drank seawater and went mad. Others simply let go. The sun burned them by day; the cold took them at night. Of the 879 who had survived the sinking, only 317 were pulled from the water when the USS Bassett finally found them on August 2.

That meant 562 men died in the water—more than died in the actual sinking. It remains the worst naval disaster in U.S. Navy history, a monument to the gap between confidence and reality. Captain McVay was court-martialed for the loss of his ship, making him one of only two commanding officers in Navy history to face such a trial for a vessel sunk in combat. He was convicted, though his sentence was later remitted. Twenty years later, in 1968, he took his own life.

The Indianapolis had carried the components of humanity's most destructive weapon to the moment of its use. Then she sank, and the Navy forgot about her crew for four days while they died in the dark.

Execution of Tsar Nicholas II and Family

18 July 1918 Yekaterinburg, Russia

The Tsar's family didn't die because the revolution demanded it. They died because someone forgot to send a telegraph.

On the night of July 16, 1918, in a basement room in Yekaterinburg, a Bolshevik execution squad shot Tsar Nicholas II, his wife Alexandra, their five children—Olga, Tatiana, Maria, Anastasia, and Alexei—along with four servants and the family doctor. Eleven people. Eleven bullets in the dark. It took less than three minutes.

But here's what no one talks about: the Bolsheviks didn't actually want to execute them that night. Not yet, anyway.

The Russian Civil War was collapsing into chaos. The Whites—anti-Bolshevik forces—were advancing toward Yekaterinburg, where the imperial family had been held under house arrest for months in a merchant's mansion called the Ipatiev House. Moscow had issued vague orders about "preventing the Tsar from escaping," but nothing definitive. Execute them? Move them? Hold them as hostages? The messages were ambiguous enough that a careful commander might have waited for clarification.

Yakov Yurovsky, the local Bolshevik commander, received a telegraph on July 16. It said the Whites would reach the city within days. He panicked. Rather than risk the family falling into enemy hands—which would have turned them into living symbols of counter-revolution—he decided to act. He didn't wait for Moscow's permission. He didn't even try to get it. He simply informed his superiors after the fact: the deed was done.

Lenin's government accepted it with the mildest bureaucratic shrug. A decision that would echo through the twentieth century was made in a basement by a frightened man trying to solve an immediate problem.

The strange part? Almost nobody at the time cared. The Russian press barely covered it. Western newspapers ran the story and moved on. The Bolsheviks didn't publicize it as a revolutionary triumph or a necessary evil—they treated it like an inconvenient administrative matter. For years, the details remained murky. Some people didn't even believe the entire family had been killed.

What made the execution stick in history wasn't the killing itself. It was what came after: the cult of the martyred Tsar. Monarchists, religious believers, and exiles transformed Nicholas from a mediocre, stubborn ruler into a tragic symbol. His daughter Anastasia became the subject of countless legends—rumors that she'd survived, that she'd escaped to America. (She hadn't. The bodies were buried in a forest and left undisturbed for decades.)

The Bolsheviks had meant to eliminate a problem. Instead, they'd created a ghost that would haunt Russian politics for generations. They killed a man to prevent his resurrection, and in doing so, ensured it.

Yurovsky lived another twenty years, dying of natural causes in 1938. He never expressed regret. He'd simply done what he thought necessary on a night when the future felt like it was closing in. History, as it turns out, often hinges on men who are too frightened to wait.
