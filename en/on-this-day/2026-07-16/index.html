Trinity: The First Atomic Bomb

16 July 1945 Alamogordo, New Mexico, USA

Robert Oppenheimer stood in the control bunker twenty miles away and watched the thing he had built vaporize the desert. It was 5:29 and 45 seconds in the morning on July 16, 1945, and the age of innocence—whatever remained of it in 1945—ended in a flash so bright that a blind woman standing ten miles away reported seeing light.

The countdown had begun at 4:45 a.m. The device itself, a sphere of plutonium wrapped in conventional explosives, hung from a 100-foot steel tower in the pre-dawn darkness. It had a name: Trinity. Nobody has ever quite explained why. Oppenheimer later said he was thinking of a John Donne sonnet about lovers, which is the kind of thing only a theoretical physicist would say about a nuclear weapon, and only after the fact.

The scientists had been arguing about what would happen. Edward Teller thought the bomb might ignite the atmosphere. Enrico Fermi, the kind of man who could calculate odds in his head while eating lunch, was taking bets on whether it would. The stakes were cosmic: would a chain reaction spiral outward and incinerate New Mexico? Would it burn through the continent? One physicist bought insurance. Another brought a camera. They were about to detonate a weapon that, if it worked at all, would work differently than anything humans had ever made.

At 5:29, the timer reached zero. The explosion bloomed upward in a column of fire—a color nobody had a word for yet. The fireball expanded at the speed of light itself, turning night into noon. The shock wave moved outward at the speed of sound, pressing flat against the earth. When it reached the bunker, it knocked men sideways. The sound arrived a few seconds later, a roar that rattled teeth.

The mushroom cloud climbed to 40,000 feet. Oppenheimer watched it rise and remembered a line from the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." He said this quietly, to almost nobody, years afterward. At the moment itself, he said nothing. He lit a cigarette and watched the desert glow.

The blast had vaporized the tower. The heat had turned the sand beneath the device into glass—a pale green glass that would stay radioactive for decades. At ground zero, nothing remained but a crater and a faint smell of ozone. The explosion released energy equal to roughly 20,000 tons of TNT. It was, by a comfortable margin, the most powerful thing any human being had ever deliberately set in motion.

Within hours, Oppenheimer sent a telegram to the Secretary of War: "Baby is born." Within months, two of these devices would be loaded onto a modified B-29 bomber and flown across the Pacific. Within seconds of the Trinity explosion—though nobody in that bunker knew this yet—the war with Japan had, in practical terms, already ended. The only question was whether anyone would ever use what they had just learned how to make.

Oppenheimer lived another seventeen years. He spent much of that time arguing that nuclear weapons should be controlled internationally, that the genie could not and should not be put back in the bottle. He was, by most measures, right. He was also, by most measures, ignored.

Srebrenica Massacre Begins

16 July 1995 Srebrenica, Bosnia and Herzegovina

On the morning of July 11, 1995, a Dutch soldier named Thom Karremans stood in the courtyard of a factory in Srebrenica and watched his own authority evaporate. He was a major commanding 400 lightly armed peacekeepers, tasked with protecting a "safe area" that now held 40,000 people. The Bosnian Serb forces surrounding the enclave numbered in the thousands, equipped with artillery and tanks. Karremans had asked his superiors for air support. He was told no. That afternoon, he wept.

Srebrenica was supposed to be untouchable. In 1993, the United Nations had declared it a safe haven for Bosniak Muslims fleeing the war that had consumed Yugoslavia's corpse. The Serbs agreed to the arrangement—or seemed to. For two years, the pocket of land held, a fragile bubble of mercy in a region being methodically cleansed of its Muslim population. Then, in early July 1995, the Serbs simply walked in.

What happened next was not spontaneous. It was bureaucratic. General Ratko Mladic, the Bosnian Serb commander, issued orders. Soldiers moved through the town in clusters. The men and boys—roughly 8,000 of them, though the number would take years to confirm—were separated from the women and children with the casual efficiency of a livestock operation. Some were taken from their homes. Others were plucked from a refugee column trudging toward Muslim-held territory. A 24-year-old named Hakija Meholjic watched his father loaded onto a bus. He never saw him again.

The killings began almost immediately, in small groups and large ones. Execution sites dotted the landscape: warehouses, schools, fields. The Serb soldiers did not hide what they were doing. They filmed some of it. One soldier, a man named Dragan Nikolic, took a photograph of himself posing above a row of corpses—a souvenir, casual as a vacation snapshot. The forensic teams would find him later, in the evidence, smiling.

What made Srebrenica singular was not the scale—larger massacres had already occurred in the Balkans—but the witnesses. Thom Karremans saw it. Dutch soldiers saw it. Journalists were there. The world was watching, and the world did almost nothing. The American ambassador in the region knew what was happening within hours. The UN knew. They had cameras, testimony, location data. And they watched the buses leave.

It would take another decade for the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia to begin prosecuting those responsible. Mladic himself remained free for sixteen years, living openly in Belgrade while the tribunal issued warrants. When he was finally arrested in 2011, he was found in a safe house with his daughter, who was a dentist. She had been treating patients the whole time.

Today, Srebrenica is a memorial. Thousands of white headstones cover a hillside. Each year, the families return to bury the identified remains of their relatives—a burial that may happen fifteen or twenty years after the death. In 2015, on the twentieth anniversary, 136 newly identified victims were laid to rest. The work continues. There are still bodies being found, still DNA being processed, still mothers and daughters waiting to bring their dead home.
