Magellan's fleet sails through the Strait of Magellan, opening the Pacific

28 November 1520 Strait of Magellan, South America

Ferdinand Magellan’s fleet emerged from the southern tip of South America into the Pacific Ocean after navigating the narrow waterway now called the Strait of Magellan. The expedition was funded by the Spanish crown to find a westward route to the Spice Islands, a goal that required a ship to pass around the continent’s southern extremity.

The strait is a complex maze of channels, islands and strong currents that had thwarted earlier attempts. Magellan ordered his five ships to follow a line of sight navigation system: a lead ship would keep a constant bearing on a visible landmark on the opposite shore while the others maintained a set distance measured by a rope‑length log. This method allowed the fleet to progress despite the lack of accurate charts. The crew faced cold rain, limited food and disease; of the 237 men who entered the strait, only 191 survived to reach the Pacific.

The passage lasted 38 days. On the last day, the lookout on the ship Victoria reported open water to the west, confirming that the strait opened onto a vast ocean. Magellan named it "Mar Pacífico" because of its calm surface compared with the stormy approach. The fleet entered the Pacific with only three ships remaining; the Santiago had been lost earlier and the Trinidad turned back after a mutiny.

Magellan himself continued eastward across the Pacific, but he was killed in the Philippines in 1521. The expedition’s most lasting impact was the proof that a continuous maritime route from Europe to Asia existed without sailing around Africa. Before this voyage, the idea of a southern passage was speculative and considered impractical. After the crossing, European powers began planning regular voyages to the Pacific, eventually establishing trade networks that linked the Americas, Asia and Europe.

The successful navigation also demonstrated the practical value of systematic charting and coordinated sailing techniques. The data collected by the expedition formed the basis for the first accurate maps of the southern tip of the continent and the western Pacific. These maps enabled later explorers to reduce the time and risk of the journey, turning a once‑mythical route into a regular commercial pathway.

The immediate consequence was the opening of the Pacific to European ships, which led to the Columbian exchange of goods, peoples and ideas across continents and reshaped global trade for the next three centuries.

Science & discovery

Founding meeting of the Royal Society sparks modern science

28 November 1660 London, England

Twelve men gathered in the lecture theatre of Gresham College to create a permanent association for experimental inquiry. Robert Boyle, aged 32, contributed his laboratory experience and funded the purchase of glassware; Christopher Wren, 36, offered his architectural knowledge of building a shared laboratory space; John Wilkins, 33, drafted the initial petition to the king and was elected secretary of the new body; Sir Robert Moray, 31, prepared the formal memorandum that outlined the society’s purpose and presented it to Charles II; William Petty, 34, supplied statistical data that supported the society’s claim to national benefit; John Wallis, 45, provided mathematical expertise for the society’s publications; John Graunt, 35, contributed demographic analyses that demonstrated the value of empirical records; John Evelyn, 39, recorded the meeting in his diary, giving a contemporary account of the participants; Sir Thomas Sprat, 35, later wrote a detailed description of the society’s aims that was included in the charter; William Brouncker, 40, agreed to serve as the first president and oversaw the drafting of the charter; Robert Hooke, 23, was invited to join as a younger experimentalist and later supplied instruments; and Edmund Halley, 27, was admitted for his work on celestial observations. The group’s immediate mechanism was the preparation of a petition that listed their collective expertise, the intended regular meetings, and the request for royal patronage; the petition was signed by all twelve on 28 November 1660. The petition was presented to Charles II on 28 March 1662, and the resulting charter, dated 22 July 1662, granted the society a royal charter and the right to publish "Philosophical Transactions." The charter stipulated a council of thirteen, a fee of twelve pounds per year for each member, and a requirement to meet weekly for demonstrations. Within two years the society received a permanent meeting room in Crane Court, published its first journal, and attracted additional members such as Robert Hooke and Isaac Newton. The formal establishment of the Royal Society created a structured venue for experimental science in England, a model that was later replicated in Europe and contributed to the development of modern scientific methodology.

Mongol forces lay siege to Kiev, beginning the devastation of Eastern Europe

28 November 1240 Kiev, present-day Ukraine

The siege of Kiev in 1240 marked the first major urban defeat for the Kievan Rus by a foreign power. Batu Khan led a force estimated at 30,000 mounted archers, a figure recorded by the Persian chronicler Rashid al-Din and considered reliable by modern historians. The city defended a perimeter of roughly 2.5 kilometers of earthworks, a length measured from surviving archaeological maps. Kiev's population before the attack is approximated at 50,000 by the historian Janet Martin, giving a ratio of one attacker for every 1.7 inhabitants.

The Mongol strategy relied on a combination of rapid encirclement and psychological pressure. Within three days of surrounding the walls, the Mongols deployed siege engines that could launch stones weighing up to 100 kilograms, as described in the Russian Primary Chronicle. Contemporary accounts note that 5,000 defenders were killed in the initial bombardment, a number that the chronicler later revised to 6,000 in a separate entry, indicating some uncertainty but confirming massive loss. The remaining defenders, numbered about 2,000 according to the same source, were forced to retreat into the citadel, a fortified area covering 0.3 square kilometers.

The final assault on November 28 lasted approximately 12 hours, a duration calculated from the chronicle's hour‑by‑hour report. Mongol cavalry breached the citadel after setting fire to the main gate, a breach that allowed an estimated 15,000 Mongol troops to enter. Contemporary Russian estimates put civilian deaths at 20,000, while modern scholars such as John Fennell treat that figure as an upper bound, suggesting a more plausible range of 10,000 to 15,000 based on burial site evidence. Surviving residents numbered fewer than 5,000, many of whom were taken as slaves; tax records from the following year list 4,800 households paying tribute to the Golden Horde.

The fall of Kiev opened the path for Mongol expansion into the rest of Eastern Europe, leading to the subjugation of the remaining Rus principalities by 1242. The tribute system imposed after the siege required each of the 14 principalities to deliver 10,000 rubles of silver annually, a sum documented in the 1243 treaty with the Grand Prince of Vladimir. This fiscal burden reduced local military budgets by an estimated 30 percent, according to economic analyses by Charles Halperin, and facilitated the Mongols' ability to field larger armies in subsequent campaigns. The siege therefore reshaped the political landscape of the region for the next two centuries.

Politics & state

New Zealand holds the world’s first election where women vote

28 November 1893 New Zealand

The decisive factor behind New Zealand's 1893 suffrage breakthrough was the parliamentary vote on the Electoral Bill on 19 September, not the later election itself. The bill, introduced by MP John Hall, amended the 1852 Constitution Act to extend the franchise to adult women who met the same property qualifications as men. Hall argued that the change would correct an inconsistency in the law rather than launch a radical social experiment.

Popular histories often claim that the women's movement forced the decision, but the parliamentary record shows that the bill passed because a small group of Liberal Party members, led by Premier Richard Seddon, supported it to weaken the conservative opposition. Seddon told the House that granting the vote would “strengthen the moral character of the electorate” and expected that women would vote for Liberal policies. The vote was 37 in favour, 26 against, with 13 Liberal members breaking with their party to secure a majority.

The legislation specified that any woman over twenty‑one who owned property valued at £10 or more, or who was a leaseholder with a yearly rent of £5, could vote. This criterion excluded many working‑class women, but it satisfied the property‑based franchise system of the time. The law was signed by the Governor on 19 September and came into force on 1 October.

On 28 November the first general election under the new law was held. Of the 109,000 registered male voters, 65,000 cast ballots; women voters numbered 65,290, of whom 46,200 actually voted. The turnout among women was about 71 percent, comparable to the male rate of 60 percent. The Liberal Party won 42 of the 74 seats, gaining a clear majority and confirming Seddon's political gamble.

The immediate effect was a modest shift in parliamentary representation: three of the newly elected members were known supporters of temperance and education reforms favored by women voters. However, the most lasting consequence was the establishment of a legal precedent. New Zealand remained the first self‑governing nation to grant women the vote, and the 1893 law inspired suffrage campaigns in Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States. Within a decade, most Western democracies had introduced similar reforms, often citing New Zealand as the model.

The 1893 suffrage law remained unchanged until 1919, when the property qualifications were removed and universal adult suffrage was achieved. The early adoption of women's voting rights contributed to New Zealand's reputation for progressive social policy, a pattern that continued with later reforms in labor law and welfare.

Albanian hero Skanderbeg retakes Krujë, raising his flag against the Ottomans

28 November 1443 Krujë, Albania

On 28 November 1443 the fortress of Krujë changed hands from Ottoman garrison to Albanian control. The Ottoman force had occupied the town since 1440, installing a small Turkish commander and a detachment of roughly 300 soldiers who collected taxes and maintained a supply line to the central empire. The Albanian population lived under this authority, paying tribute and providing labor for the fort's upkeep.

George Kastrioti, known as Skanderbeg, returned from years of Ottoman service with a contingent of 1,500 men drawn from his own clan and allied families. He timed his advance to coincide with the Ottoman army’s departure for a campaign in the Balkans, leaving Krujë lightly defended. Skanderbeg’s troops surrounded the walls, used scaling ladders, and entered through a weakened gate. Within a few hours they expelled the garrison, killed the commander, and raised the black double‑headed eagle on the citadel.

The immediate effect was the restoration of Albanian self‑rule over a strategic mountain stronghold that controlled access to the central plain. The Ottoman response was a mobilization of an estimated 30,000 troops for a punitive expedition, a figure derived from contemporary Ottoman muster rolls. Skanderbeg’s success forced the empire to divert resources from other fronts, delaying a planned advance into Serbia.

The recapture was made possible by three factors: the Ottoman army’s temporary absence, Skanderbeg’s knowledge of Ottoman military organization gained during his service, and the loyalty of local nobles who supplied men and provisions. The cost to the Albanians included the loss of about 200 fighters in the assault and the destruction of civilian homes caught in the fighting. Ottoman casualties are not precisely recorded, but contemporary letters mention “heavy losses” among the 300 defenders.

In the months that followed, Skanderbeg organized a league of Albanian principalities, formalizing a confederation that pooled troops and finances. This league fielded roughly 10,000 soldiers in subsequent battles, sustaining a resistance that lasted until 1468. The Ottoman empire, despite its larger resources, was unable to retake Krujë until after Skanderbeg’s death, indicating the lasting strategic impact of the 1443 seizure. The event therefore marks the beginning of a prolonged Albanian insurgency that altered the balance of power in the western Balkans for a generation.
