The Battle of France begins during World War II

3 June 1940 France

As the sun rises over the French countryside on May 10, 1940, the sound of artillery fire and screams of terrified civilians fill the air, marking the beginning of the Battle of France. The German army, led by Adolf Hitler, has launched a surprise attack on France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, catching the Allied forces off guard. The French military, commanded by General Maurice Gamelin, is struggling to respond to the rapid German advance, with the enemy already pushing deep into French territory.

The situation before the battle was tense, with Germany having already invaded Poland in September 1939, prompting the United Kingdom and France to declare war. The French military had been preparing for a traditional warfare scenario, with a strong emphasis on defense, while the German army had been secretly developing a new tactic called the 'blitzkrieg,' which involved rapid advances and surprise attacks. As the German army advances, the French air force is quickly overwhelmed, with over 1,000 French planes destroyed in the first few days of the battle.

As the battle rages on, the French army is in disarray, with many units retreating in chaos. The German army, on the other hand, is moving swiftly, with its tanks and infantry advancing up to 20 miles per day. The French government, led by Prime Minister Paul Reynaud, is in crisis mode, with many officials calling for a ceasefire. On June 3, 1940, the German air force launches a massive bombing campaign against Paris, killing over 200 civilians and destroying much of the city. The French army is now in full retreat, with many soldiers fleeing in panic.

The human side of the battle is one of fear, chaos, and desperation. French civilians are fleeing their homes, trying to escape the advancing German army. Many are trapped, with no way to escape, and are forced to endure the brutal conditions of war. The soldiers on both sides are exhausted, hungry, and scared, with many suffering from injuries and illnesses. As the battle reaches its climax, the French army is on the brink of collapse, with over 100,000 soldiers killed or wounded.

The science behind the German army's success lies in its use of the blitzkrieg tactic, which involves rapid advances and surprise attacks. The German army has developed a new type of tank, the Panzer, which is faster and more maneuverable than traditional tanks. The Panzer tanks are supported by infantry and air power, allowing the German army to quickly overwhelm the French defenses. The French army, on the other hand, is using traditional warfare tactics, which are no match for the German blitzkrieg.

As the battle comes to an end, the French government is forced to sign the Armistice of Compiègne, which effectively surrenders France to Germany. The armistice is signed on June 22, 1940, in the same railway car where the Germans surrendered in 1918. The irony of this moment is not lost on the French people, who are forced to endure the humiliation of defeat in the same location where their ancestors had once triumphed. The consequences of the battle are devastating, with France being divided into two zones, one under German occupation and the other under the control of the French collaborationist government. The battle marks the beginning of a long and brutal occupation, which will last for four years and claim the lives of millions of French civilians.

Maurice Herzog and Louis Lachenal reach the summit of Annapurna

3 June 1950 Himalayas, Nepal

As Maurice Herzog and Louis Lachenal stood at the summit of Annapurna, the first 8000-meter peak to be conquered, on June 03, 1950, they were not just celebrating a historic achievement, but also overcoming a decision that had put their entire expedition at risk. The French team had chosen to climb Annapurna without proper reconnaissance, a decision that would later be questioned by many. Herzog, then 31 years old, had been planning this expedition for years, and his determination to reach the summit was driven by a sense of national pride and a desire to prove that the French could conquer the highest peaks in the world.

The situation before the climb was far from ideal. The team had arrived in Nepal in March 1950, with limited knowledge of the mountain and its terrain. They had to navigate through dense forests, cross raging rivers, and climb steep slopes to reach the base camp. The ascent was further complicated by the fact that the team had to establish a series of higher camps, using local porters to carry their equipment and supplies. Herzog and Lachenal, along with their teammate Lionel Terray, had climbed to an altitude of over 24,000 feet, where the air was thin and the temperatures were well below freezing.

The climb to the summit was a grueling and treacherous journey. Herzog and Lachenal had to use ice axes and crampons to traverse the icy slopes, and they had to be extremely careful to avoid avalanches and crevasses. The duo climbed for over 12 hours, covering a distance of around 3 miles, to reach the summit at 2 pm local time. As they stood at the top, they were met with a stunning view of the Himalayas, with the sun shining brightly in the sky. Herzog would later describe the moment as 'a sense of perfect joy, a joy that I had never felt before.'

The achievement was not without its costs. Both Herzog and Lachenal suffered severe frostbite, and they had to be evacuated from the mountain as quickly as possible. The descent was equally challenging, with the team having to navigate through the same difficult terrain they had climbed up. Herzog would later have to undergo several surgeries to amputate his toes, which had been severely damaged by frostbite. Despite the risks and challenges, the team's achievement marked a significant milestone in the history of mountaineering, and it paved the way for future expeditions to the highest peaks in the world.

The irony of Herzog's achievement lies in the fact that he never climbed again after Annapurna. The injuries he sustained during the expedition left him with a lifelong disability, and he had to abandon his dreams of becoming a professional mountaineer. As he looked back at his achievement, Herzog would later say, 'I had reached the summit, but at what cost?'
