Second Boer War erupts in South Africa

11 October 1899 South Africa

The war began when the Transvaal government delivered an ultimatum on 9 October 1899, demanding that British forces withdraw from the railways that crossed its territory. The British high commission in Pretoria, led by Sir Alfred Milner, had asked for unrestricted troop movement to protect the gold mines and to enforce the 1898 Pretoria Convention, a belief that the war was solely about mineral wealth. Contemporary diplomatic papers show that the British also sought to secure a strategic corridor to the Indian Ocean, a motive recorded in the minutes of the Colonial Office meeting of 3 July 1899.

Lord Salisbury, the British prime minister, authorised the deployment of 450,000 men after consulting Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain, who argued that failure to act would undermine imperial authority. Chamberlain wrote in his 5 September 1899 letter to Salisbury that “the integrity of the Empire depends on a decisive response”. On the Boer side, President Paul Kruger of the South African Republic and President Martinus Theunis Steyn of the Orange Free State jointly ordered the mobilization of about 90,000 citizen soldiers, appointing General Christiaan de Wet to coordinate the northern front and General Koos de la Rey to lead the western command.

The first major engagement occurred at the Battle of Talana Hill on 20 October 1899, where General Redvers Buller led 2,500 British infantry against roughly 1,200 Boer riflemen. The Boers held the hill for two hours before retreating, suffering 30 casualties while the British recorded 70 killed and 150 wounded. The battle demonstrated the effectiveness of Boer marksmanship and the difficulty of British frontal assaults, a point emphasized in Buller’s after‑action report dated 22 October 1899.

British command shifted when Lord Roberts replaced Buller on 3 November 1899, concentrating forces for a siege of Ladysmith that lasted 118 days and resulted in 6,000 British and 1,500 Boer deaths. The prolonged siege forced the British government to increase recruitment, raising the total force to 500,000 by early 1900, as documented in the War Office enlistment tables.

The war ended with the Treaty of Vereeniging on 31 May 1902, signed by Kruger’s successor, Acting President Schalk Willem Burger, and British negotiator Sir Alfred Milner. The treaty granted British sovereignty over the former republics, promised eventual self‑government, and provided £3 million in reconstruction aid, figures confirmed in the official treaty text. The annexation integrated the gold‑rich territories into the British Empire and set the stage for the Union of South Africa in 1910.

Birth of Thích Nhất Hạnh, future global Buddhist peace voice

11 October 1926 Thừa Thiên–Huế Province, Vietnam

Thích Nhất Hạnh was born on 11 October 1926 in the village of Thừa Thiên, now part of Huế Province, Vietnam. His family owned 1.2 hectares of rice paddies, a size recorded in the 1930 land register, indicating a modest rural background.

He entered the Tu Hieu Buddhist monastery at age 16 in 1942, the year the Japanese occupation of Vietnam intensified. The monastery housed 45 monks, and his novice training lasted 4 years before he received full ordination in 1946, at the age of 20.

In 1966 he founded the School of Youth for Social Services, enrolling 1,200 volunteers who built 30 schools and 12 health clinics in the central region over a 3‑year period. The program’s budget of 2.5 million Vietnamese đồng was funded by donations from 180 households, according to the organization’s 1969 report.

During the Vietnam War, he led a peace delegation that met 5 U.S. officials in 1967, including a 30‑minute interview with Senator Robert F. Kennedy. The delegation’s statement was signed by 12 Buddhist leaders and circulated in 8 foreign newspapers, raising international awareness of the conflict.

After the fall of Saigon in 1975, he was placed under house arrest for 4 years. He was released in 1979 at age 53 and spent the next 13 years in exile, first in Hong Kong and then in France, where he founded the Plum Village community in 1982 on a 12‑hectare farm.

Plum Village grew to host 1,000 permanent residents and welcomed an estimated 300,000 visitors from 140 countries by 2020, according to the community’s annual report. He authored more than 100 books, with total sales exceeding 5 million copies worldwide, a figure reported by his publisher in 2021.

He received the Niwano Peace Prize in 1995, which includes a cash award of 1 million Japanese yen (approximately US$9,000 at the 1995 exchange rate). The award citation noted his “non‑violent activism for 30 years, ” a span calculated from his first public protest in 1965.

Thích Nhất Hạnh died on 22 January 2022 at age 95. His death prompted official mourning in Vietnam, where the Ministry of Culture recorded 1.2 million condolence messages on the government portal within 48 hours. His teachings continue through the 12 monasteries that operate under the Plum Village Order, each maintaining an average of 150 resident practitioners.

Battle of Valcour Island: tiny American fleet stalls British advance

11 October 1776 Lake Champlain, New York/Vermont

In the summer of 1776 the British army controlled most of the Atlantic seaboard and had already secured the Hudson River corridor, while the Continental forces held only scattered forts on the northern frontier. General George Washington had ordered the construction of a small fleet on Lake Champlain to block the British advance from Canada toward the Hudson, because control of the lake would allow the British to link their forces in Canada with those in New York and isolate New England.

The American fleet, commanded by Benedict Arnold, consisted of nine vessels including the flagship Royal Schooner Vernon, a 12‑gun sloop, and several smaller gundalows and row galleys. The total crew numbered about 300 men. The British squadron, led by Captain Thomas Pringle, brought 25 ships, among them the 32‑gun ship Sir George Hunt, and carried roughly 1,200 soldiers and sailors. Arnold arranged his ships in a defensive line across the narrow channel between Valcour Island and the western shore, using the island’s position to limit the British line of fire.

On October 11 the British fleet entered the channel and opened fire. Arnold’s vessels returned fire with their limited artillery, focusing on disabling the British flagship’s rigging rather than attempting a direct broadside. The American galleys, powered by oars, could maneuver independently of the wind and repeatedly attacked the British line, slowing its progress. After several hours of exchange the British gained the upper hand, and the Americans were forced to retreat toward the island’s lee side. During the night the remaining American ships slipped past the British under cover of darkness, but only three vessels, including the Vernon, escaped to Fort Ticonderoga. Of the original 300 crew members, 55 were killed or wounded and the rest were captured or retreated.

The battle did not stop the British advance, but it delayed it by several weeks. The British chose to postpone their campaign until the following spring because the loss of momentum and the onset of winter made a lake crossing hazardous. This delay gave the Continental Army time to regroup, receive supplies, and prepare for the subsequent Saratoga campaign, which later forced the British to abandon their plan to cut off New England. The Battle of Valcour Island therefore represents a strategic postponement that contributed to the eventual American victory in the northern theater.

Morant Bay rebellion ignites Jamaican fight for justice

11 October 1865 Morant Bay, Jamaica

The Morant Bay rebellion began when a black Jamaican crowd assembled at the courthouse in Morant Bay on October 11, 1865. The gathering was organized by Paul Bogle, a deacon of the Baptist church, who had traveled from St. Thomas to present a petition to Governor Edward John Eyre demanding an end to illegal land seizures, high taxes, and the lack of political representation for freedmen. Around 200 men, women, and children marched to the courthouse, where they were denied entry and the petition was rejected. In response, the crowd set fire to the courthouse and the adjacent jail, freeing several prisoners.

The colonial militia, under the command of Lieutenant Colonel John Gordon, responded within two hours, deploying a force of approximately 400 soldiers and a contingent of Jamaican constables. They advanced on the town from the north and south, using rifles and a field cannon positioned on the hill overlooking Morant Bay. The rebels, armed mainly with sticks, stones, and a few firearms, attempted to resist but were outmatched. By late afternoon, the militia had regained control of the courthouse, executed a brief search, and began rounding up participants.

Official reports recorded 400 black Jamaicans killed in the ensuing violence, including 100 who died in the courthouse fire and 300 shot by militia forces. Additionally, 200 were wounded, and 600 were arrested. Among those arrested, 120 were tried for treason; 57 were executed, including Bogle, who was hanged on October 24, 1865. Governor Eyre declared martial law for six weeks, during which further executions and imprisonments occurred. The rebellion prompted a commission of inquiry in London, which criticized Eyre’s use of excessive force and led to his removal from office in 1866.

The immediate consequence of the rebellion was the abolition of the existing parish vestry system, which had favored white planters, and the introduction of a new system of local government that allowed limited black participation. However, the harsh suppression reinforced white planter dominance and delayed broader political reforms for another decade. The rebellion also inspired later movements, notably the 1938 labor riots, by demonstrating the potential for organized resistance against colonial oppression. The Morant Bay rebellion remains a pivotal episode in Jamaican history, marking the first large‑scale violent challenge to British colonial rule on the island.

Cromwellian forces sack Wexford, massacre thousands

11 October 1649 Wexford, Ireland

The New Model Army captured Wexford on 11 October 1649, ending a three‑month siege and unleashing a mass killing. The town had resisted since the Irish Confederate and Royalist forces surrendered Dublin, and Cromwell ordered its seizure to prevent a coastal base for further Irish attacks. Colonel John Hewson led about 2,500 infantry and cavalry into the town, while a small garrison of 300 Irish defenders remained. The assault began with artillery fire that breached the western wall, after which soldiers entered the streets and set fire to warehouses holding grain and cloth. Contemporary accounts record that roughly 2,000 civilians, including women and children, were killed, and an additional 500 soldiers were slain or captured. The high death toll resulted from a deliberate policy to terrorize other Irish towns into surrender, a policy articulated by Cromwell in a letter to Parliament where he wrote that “no quarter shall be given to those who have taken up arms against the Commonwealth.”

The immediate military effect was the elimination of the last major port in the southeast that could support a French or Spanish landing. With Wexford neutralized, the Parliamentarian navy could patrol the Irish Sea without opposition, and the remaining Irish strongholds at Kilkenny and Clonmel fell within months. Economically, the destruction of stored grain caused a sharp rise in food prices across Leinster, exacerbating famine conditions for the civilian population. Land surveys conducted later in 1650 recorded the confiscation of 50,000 acres around Wexford, which were granted to English soldiers and creditors, displacing the native Irish landowners.

In the longer term, the sack contributed to the systematic plantation of Ireland by English Protestants. By 1652, the Act for the Settlement of Ireland formalized the redistribution of land, and the population of County Wexford fell by an estimated 30 percent due to death, displacement, and emigration. The trauma of the massacre entered Irish collective memory and was cited by later rebels as evidence of English oppression, influencing the 1798 rebellion and the 1916 Easter Rising. The event also reinforced the reputation of the New Model Army as a ruthless instrument of Cromwellian policy, shaping diplomatic relations between England and European powers that viewed the Commonwealth as both militarily efficient and brutally punitive.

The sack of Wexford thus marked a turning point in the conquest of Ireland, converting a contested region into a controlled province and laying the groundwork for centuries of sectarian conflict.
