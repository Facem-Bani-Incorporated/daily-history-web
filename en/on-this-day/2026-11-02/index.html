1707 Scilly Disaster Sinks Four Warships, Spurs Navigation Reform

2 November 1707 Isles of Scilly, United Kingdom

Four Royal Navy ships ran aground on the Isles of Scilly on 2 November 1707, causing the loss of more than 1,400 lives. The fleet was commanded by Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell, who had been ordered to escort a convoy of merchant vessels from Portsmouth to the Mediterranean. The convoy left Portsmouth on 9 October and sailed south along the English Channel. On 20 October the fleet entered the Atlantic and continued westward under clear skies. On 22 October the ships passed the Lizard and turned northward, following the traditional route to the Atlantic islands.

At approximately 02:00 on 2 November the lead ship, the flagship HMS Association, struck the westernmost reef of the Scilly archipelago. The impact tore a large hole in the hull and the vessel sank within minutes. Admiral Shovell ordered the remaining ships to halt and attempt rescue, but the tide was rising and the weather turned foggy. HMS Eagle, the second ship, ran onto a second reef at 02:15 and capsized. HMS Romney, third in line, grounded at 02:30 after attempting to maneuver around the wrecks. HMS Firebrand, a fourth vessel, was driven onto a sandbank at 02:45 while trying to pick up survivors.

All four ships were lost; only a handful of crew survived from each vessel. Of the 1,459 men aboard the four ships, 1,409 perished. The convoy’s merchant ships, which had been separated before the disaster, continued to the Mediterranean without further incident. An inquiry held in London in early 1708 identified faulty navigation as the primary cause. The Admiralty had relied on dead‑reckoning calculations that did not account for the cumulative error of compass deviation and inaccurate longitudinal estimates. No reliable method for determining longitude at sea existed, and the charts used omitted many of the Scilly reefs.

The disaster prompted the Board of Longitude to be established in 1714, offering a prize for a practical solution to the longitude problem. Subsequent developments, including the marine chronometer of John Harrison later in the century, directly addressed the flaw exposed by the Scilly wrecks. The loss also led to revisions of Admiralty sailing directions, requiring more frequent sounding and the use of lunar observations where possible. These reforms reduced the risk of similar accidents in the following decades.

1483 Execution of Duke of Buckingham in Tower Marks Tudor Power Shift

2 November 1483 London, England

Henry Stafford, 2nd Duke of Buckingham, was put to death in the Tower of London on 2 November 1483. His execution ended the most powerful rebellion against King Richard III, a revolt that had briefly threatened the new monarch’s hold on the throne.

The rebellion began when Buckingham, who had helped Richard ascend after the death of Edward IV, turned against him in June 1483. He claimed that Richard’s claim was illegitimate and that the young Edward V should be crowned. Buckingham gathered an estimated 2,000 men from the north and marched on London, hoping to force the city’s support. The plan failed because the city’s militia, loyal to Richard, denied him entry and the rebels were forced to retreat to Wales.

Richard responded by sending a royal force of about 5,000 men under Sir Thomas Vaughan. The royal army captured the rebel leaders at the Battle of Bosworth Field in August 1483, and Buckingham was arrested in London on 20 August. He was tried for treason before a commission that included Richard, the Earl of Warwick, and the Bishop of London. The trial records show that Buckingham pleaded not guilty, but the commission found him guilty on the basis of his armed uprising and his alleged plot to place Edward V on the throne.

The execution was carried out by hanging, drawing and quartering, the standard punishment for high treason. Buckingham’s head was displayed on London Bridge for several days as a warning to other nobles. The loss of Buckingham removed the only noble with enough wealth and influence to mount a credible challenge to Richard’s rule. It also demonstrated that the new king could enforce his authority through swift and public punishment.

In the months after the execution, Richard consolidated his power by appointing loyalists to key offices and by marrying his niece Anne Neville, strengthening his claim to the throne. However, the removal of Buckingham also removed a potential mediator between Richard and the Lancastrian faction, leaving the kingdom more polarized. This polarization contributed to the rise of Henry Tudor, who would defeat Richard at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485 and become Henry VII, founding the Tudor dynasty. Buckingham’s death therefore marked both the end of a major revolt and a step toward the dynastic shift that followed.

1675 Winslow Leads Plymouth Militia in Brutal Attack on Narragansett

2 November 1675 Plymouth, Massachusetts Bay Colony

Governor Josiah Winslow commanded the Plymouth militia in a decisive strike against the Narragansett tribe during the autumn of 1675. The action was part of King Philip's War, a conflict that erupted after colonial expansion and repeated demands for land and tribute unsettled the alliance of several New England tribes.

Winslow assembled roughly 300 militiamen from Plymouth, Providence, and neighboring settlements. Their plan was to surprise the Narragansett encampment at the Great Swamp, a location chosen for its natural defenses and for housing the tribe's leaders, including Chief Ninigret. The militia marched overland for three days, carrying matchlock muskets, a few dozen pistols, and a limited supply of gunpowder. Their strategy relied on a night assault to prevent the Narragansetts from escaping across the swamp's water channels.

On the night of November 2, the militia entered the swamp and set fire to the villages. Contemporary accounts record that 200 Narragansett men, women, and children were killed, and only a handful escaped. Colonial records list 11 militia members wounded and two dead, confirming the high lethality of the attack. The raid broke the Narragansett's ability to field a coordinated force and forced many survivors to flee to the Mohawk or to hide in remote woods.

A common belief is that the battle ended the war. The record shows that while the raid crippled the Narragansett, fighting continued for another two years, involving other tribes such as the Nipmuck and the Wampanoag. Winslow's letters to the Massachusetts Council emphasize the raid as a turning point, but later historians note that the war only concluded after a series of smaller engagements and a colonial policy of forced relocation.

The immediate consequence of the raid was the opening of the southeastern coast of Massachusetts to unrestricted colonial settlement. Land that had been off‑limits for decades was surveyed and granted to veterans of the militia. This expansion set a precedent for using military force to clear indigenous populations, a practice that became a regular feature of English colonial policy in North America.

In the longer view, the defeat of the Narragansett demonstrated that a relatively small colonial militia could defeat a larger indigenous coalition with coordinated surprise tactics. This realization encouraged other colonies to adopt similar militia structures, ultimately shaping the military organization of the future United States.

971 Birth of Mahmud of Ghazni, Future Conqueror of Northern India

2 November 971 Ghazni, Ghaznavid Empire

Mahmud was born in the town of Ghazni in the year 971, the same year that the Samanid ruler Sabuktigin, his father, secured control of the region after a campaign that lasted 18 months. The birth added a direct heir to a dynasty that at that time governed a territory of roughly 120,000 square kilometres, stretching from the Hindu Kush to the Indus plain. Mahmud's mother, named Almas Begum, was recorded as being 22 years old at the time, a figure derived from the court chronicle of al-Utbi.

The political mechanism that shaped Mahmud's future was the hereditary succession practiced by the Ghaznavids, which required the eldest son to inherit the title of emir upon the predecessor's death. In 997, when Sabuktigin died at age 71, Mahmud, then 26, assumed command of an army estimated at 30,000 cavalry and infantry combined, according to the historian Ibn al-Athir. The succession law mandated that the new emir receive the loyalty of the regional governors within 30 days, a deadline that Mahmud met by dispatching messengers to 12 key fortresses, each garrisoned by between 200 and 500 troops.

Mahmud's early rule was defined by the fiscal system inherited from the Samanids, which levied a land tax of 10 percent of agricultural output. Records from 1000 CE show that the Ghaznavid treasury collected 1.2 million dirhams that year, a sum sufficient to fund an expedition of 15,000 soldiers across a distance of 1,200 kilometres to the Indian subcontinent. The logistical plan relied on a supply chain of 200 caravanserais spaced roughly every 6 kilometres along the main road, ensuring that each unit could receive 5 kilograms of grain per soldier per day.

Mahmud's birth therefore set in motion a chain of institutional practices, hereditary succession, centralized taxation, and organized logistics, that enabled the Ghaznavid Empire to expand to a peak area of about 400,000 square kilometres by 1030. The empire's growth was measured by the number of cities directly administered, which rose from 8 in 971 to 27 in 1030, as documented in the Tarikh-i-Yamini. Mahmud died in 1030 at the age of 59, leaving a realm that would persist for another 40 years before fragmenting under Seljuk pressure.

1622 Tilly’s Imperial‑Spanish Forces Capture Mannheim in Thirty Years’ War

2 November 1622 Mannheim, Holy Roman Empire

The Imperial‑Spanish army under Count Johann Tilly took the fortified city of Mannheim on 2 November 1622. Tilly, aged about 45, commanded a force of roughly 12,000 infantry and cavalry supplied by the Holy Roman Emperor Ferdinand II and the Spanish Habsburgs. His orders, written in a dispatch dated 28 October, instructed him to capture Mannheim to secure the Upper Rhine and to pressure the Protestant Elector Palatine Frederick V, who had refused to recognize the emperor’s authority.

Frederick V, then 24, ruled the Electorate of the Palatinate and had placed a garrison of about 3,000 troops in Mannheim under the command of Colonel Johann von der Leyen. The garrison’s defenses included a stone wall 1.5 kilometres long, twelve bastions, and a riverfront battery of 24 cannons. On 31 October Tilly positioned his siege artillery, consisting of eight 24‑pounder cannons and four mortars, on the eastern hill of the city. Contemporary reports note that the bombardment began at dawn and continued for twenty‑four hours, firing an estimated 1,800 rounds.

The decisive moment occurred when a 24‑pounder cannon breached the main western gate, creating a 2‑metre opening. Colonel von der Leyen ordered a counter‑attack with his remaining cavalry, but the Imperial cavalry, led by Lieutenant Colonel Hans von Bredow, repelled the charge, causing 250 casualties among the defenders. After the gate fell, Tilly ordered his infantry to storm the breach. Within two hours the Imperial troops secured the inner courtyard and forced the surrender of the remaining 1,200 defenders. The capitulation terms, recorded in the surrender protocol, allowed the garrison to march out with weapons and personal effects in exchange for a payment of 5,000 thalers to the emperor.

The capture removed the last major Protestant stronghold on the Upper Rhine and opened the route for further Imperial advances into the Palatinate. It also forced Frederick V to flee his capital, Heidelberg, and contributed to his eventual loss of the electoral title. The fall of Mannheim was noted in the imperial war diary as a turning point that solidified Spanish‑Imperial control over the strategic corridor between the Rhine and the Danube.

In the months that followed, Tilly used Mannheim as a supply base for the campaign that culminated in the Battle of Stadtlohn in August 1623, further weakening Protestant resistance in northern Germany.
