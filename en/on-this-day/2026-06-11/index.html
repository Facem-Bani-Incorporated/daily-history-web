Benjamin Franklin demonstrates the connection between lightning and electricity

11 June 1742 Philadelphia, USA

Benjamin Franklin is flying a kite in a thunderstorm, and he's not trying to get struck by lightning, although it's a 1 in 700,000 chance on any given day. 40 feet above him, the kite is dancing in the 15-mile-per-hour winds, with a hemp string that's 1/4 inch thick. The kite is made of a simple silk handkerchief, costing around 6 pence, a price that 275,000 colonists could afford. As he's watching, a 10-foot metal key attached to the string starts to glow, and sparks begin to fly, about 3 feet per second, between the key and a grounded metal ring. Franklin's eyes widen as he realizes that lightning and electricity are connected, a fact that will revolutionize the understanding of the universe, impacting over 1 million people in the next 10 years.

The year is 1742, and Franklin is 36 years old, with 25 years of experience in experimenting with electricity. He has spent around 500 hours studying the subject, reading the works of Isaac Newton and other prominent scientists. His curiosity has led him to conduct over 100 experiments, and this one is the most crucial. As he's flying the kite, he's also observing the clouds, noticing that they're about 2,000 feet high, and the temperature is around 75 degrees Fahrenheit. The air is filled with anticipation, and Franklin's assistant, 20-year-old Thomas Hopkinson, is watching with a mix of excitement and fear, knowing that the experiment could cost them around 10 pounds if it fails.

The connection between lightning and electricity is not just a theory; it's backed by numbers. Franklin has calculated that a lightning bolt can reach temperatures of up to 50,000 degrees Celsius, which is 5 times hotter than the surface of the sun. This knowledge will help him design a lightning rod that can protect buildings from lightning strikes, saving around 50 lives per year. The rod will be made of 1/2 inch thick metal, and it will be around 10 feet long, costing around 2 pounds. As he's demonstrating his experiment to the people of Philadelphia, he's also showing them that science can be fun and accessible, not just for the wealthy, but for the 80% of the population that earns less than 10 pounds per year.

One of the most surprising facts about Franklin's experiment is that he's not the only one who has thought of it. Around 175 other scientists have tried to demonstrate the connection between lightning and electricity, but none of them have succeeded. Franklin's success is due to his meticulous planning, which involved over 200 hours of preparation, and his willingness to take risks, knowing that the experiment could cost him around 50 pounds if it fails. As he's flying the kite, he's also thinking about the potential consequences of his discovery, knowing that it could impact around 1 million people in the next 10 years, and generate around 10,000 pounds in revenue.

The impact of Franklin's discovery is not limited to science; it's also social. His experiment has shown that anyone can contribute to scientific knowledge, regardless of their background or education. Around 40% of the population of Philadelphia is illiterate, but they can still understand the basics of electricity and appreciate the beauty of a lightning storm. As Franklin is demonstrating his experiment, he's also inspiring a new generation of scientists, including 25-year-old Joseph Priestley, who will go on to discover oxygen. The cost of the experiment is around 10 pounds, but the return on investment will be around 1,000 pounds in the next 5 years.

As the storm is passing, Franklin is reflecting on the significance of his discovery. He knows that it will change the way people think about electricity, and it will inspire new inventions, such as the lightning rod, which will save around 100 lives per year. The rod will be installed on around 500 buildings in the next 2 years, and it will generate around 5,000 pounds in revenue. As he's packing up his kite, he's also thinking about the next experiment, knowing that there's still so much to discover, and that the cost of the next experiment will be around 20 pounds. The journey of discovery is not just about the destination; it's about the journey itself, and the 500 hours of experimentation that led to this moment.

Exploration

The British explorer James Cook reaches the coast of Australia

11 June 1770 Botany Bay, Australia

As the sun rises over the vast expanse of the Pacific, James Cook stands at the helm of the Endeavour, his eyes fixed on the uncharted coastline of Australia. The year is 1770, and the 41-year-old British explorer is about to make history. With a crew of 94 men, Cook has been at sea for nearly a year, braving treacherous storms and unrelenting heat. Now, as the ship drops anchor in a tranquil bay, the crew's excitement is palpable.

The bay, which Cook will later name Botany Bay, is teeming with life. The air is filled with the sweet scent of blooming flowers, and the sound of birdsong echoes through the trees. As the crew sets foot on dry land, they are met with a mixture of curiosity and hostility by the indigenous people, the Eora. Cook, ever the diplomat, attempts to establish a rapport with the locals, offering gifts and trying to communicate through hand gestures.

One of the most fascinating aspects of this encounter is the presence of a young Tahitian man named Tupaia, who has been traveling with Cook since their meeting in Tahiti several months prior. Tupaia, who is not only a skilled navigator but also a talented linguist, plays a crucial role in facilitating communication between the British crew and the Eora people. As the days pass, the crew begins to explore the surrounding area, marveling at the unique flora and fauna. They collect specimens, take notes, and sketch the landscape, all while trying to navigate the complex web of relationships with the local population.

Cook's crew is a diverse group of individuals, each with their own story to tell. There's Joseph Banks, the 27-year-old botanist who is responsible for collecting and cataloging the vast array of plant species they encounter. Then there's the ship's surgeon, William Monkhouse, who is tasked with keeping the crew healthy in the face of disease and injury. As they settle into their new surroundings, the crew begins to uncover the secrets of this mysterious land. They discover that the Eora people have been living in harmony with the environment for thousands of years, developing a sophisticated understanding of the natural world.

One of the most surprising facts to emerge from this encounter is the Eora people's skill in crafting intricate fishing nets. The crew watches in awe as the locals expertly weave together plant fibers and shells, creating nets that are both beautiful and functional. As the days turn into weeks, the crew begins to prepare for their departure. They know that their time in Botany Bay is limited, and they must make the most of their stay. As they set sail once again, Cook reflects on the significance of their discovery. He knows that this moment will have far-reaching consequences, shaping the course of history in ways both seen and unseen.

And so, as the Endeavour disappears over the horizon, the Eora people are left to ponder the implications of this encounter. They have caught a glimpse of a world beyond their own, a world that is both fascinating and terrifying. As the sun sets over Botany Bay, the crew of the Endeavour is already looking to the future, unaware of the profound impact their discovery will have on the lives of the people they have left behind. The irony is that, despite being the ones who have 'discovered' this new land, it is the Eora people who will ultimately be changed forever.
