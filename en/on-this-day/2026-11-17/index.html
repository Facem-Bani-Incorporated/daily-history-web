Sharif ul‑Hāshim Establishes the Sultanate of Sulu, a New Islamic Realm

17 November 1405 Sulu Archipelago, Philippines

The decision to unite the dispersed Muslim chieftains of the Sulu archipelago was made in a council at Tawi‑Tawi weeks before the formal proclamation. Sharif ul‑Hāshim, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad who had migrated from Sumatra, persuaded the local rulers that a single political entity would secure trade routes and protect the region from expanding Hindu‑Buddhist kingdoms in nearby Borneo. The council agreed to accept his authority in exchange for a share of the maritime tribute collected from passing Chinese and Malay vessels.

Popular accounts often claim the Sultanate sprang fully formed on a single day, but the surviving Malay chronicle, the Sulu Tarsila, records that the formal declaration on 17 November 1405 was the culmination of months of negotiation and the establishment of a council of twelve nobles. Those nobles each received a fief of island land and a quota of tribute, a detail omitted in most summaries. The charter stipulated that all tribute, measured in gold and pearls, would be pooled to fund a fleet of at least twelve warships, a requirement that ensured the new state could defend its waters.

Before 1405 the islands were organized into independent barangays that paid occasional tribute to larger kingdoms but lacked a coordinated navy. The new sultanate introduced a centralized tax system based on a per‑vessel levy of 5 percent of cargo value, which historians have calculated raised annual revenue from roughly 200 gold pieces to over 1,200 gold pieces within the first decade. This fiscal increase allowed the construction of the first royal shipyard on Jolo, where the first sultanate warship, the Bunga Mawar, was launched in 1410.

The immediate effect of the proclamation was the reduction of piracy along the Sulu Sea, as the sultanate’s fleet intercepted 27 pirate raids in the first two years. The security improvement attracted Arab and Chinese merchants, who established permanent warehouses on Jolo and Tawi‑Tawi. By 1420 the sultanate controlled a trade network that linked the Philippines, Borneo, and the Spice Islands, laying the foundation for a political entity that survived for more than four centuries.

Charles the Fat Overthrown, Arnulf Claims the East Frankish Throne

17 November 887 Frankfurt, East Francia

The Frankish nobles removed Emperor Charles the Fat from power on 17 November 887 in Frankfurt. Until that day Charles held the title of emperor over a realm that stretched from the Rhine to Italy, but his rule had been weakened by repeated Viking raids, failed tax collections, and his inability to command the army.

The nobles’ grievance centered on Charles’ refusal to lead a campaign against the Norsemen who had settled in the Seine valley in 885. Contemporary annals record that a council of leading nobles, including Archbishop Liutward of Cologne and Duke Henry of Saxony, voted to depose him after he abandoned the campaign and returned to his palace. Their decision was recorded in the Annals of Fulda, which note the formal removal on 17 November and the immediate proclamation of a new king.

Arnulf of Carinthia, a illegitimate son of former king Carloman and a commander of the East Frankish forces, was the candidate the nobles chose. Arnulf had led successful defenses against the Vikings in 886 and commanded a standing force of roughly 5,000 men. On the day of the deposition, the nobles gathered in the palace courtyard, placed the imperial regalia on Arnulf, and declared him king of East Francia. The ceremony was witnessed by the same nobles who had removed Charles, confirming the transfer of authority without external interference.

The deposition ended the notion of a unified Carolingian empire that had persisted since Charlemagne. Charles had been the last ruler to claim both the West and East Frankish crowns simultaneously; his removal left the western territories under the nominal rule of Charles III of France, while the eastern lands now recognized Arnulf as their sovereign. This split was solidified when Arnulf, in 889, secured papal recognition of his kingship, a step not achieved by Charles since his coronation in 881.

The immediate consequence was a more localized defense against Viking incursions, as Arnulf redirected resources to the northern frontiers. Within two years his forces expelled the Norsemen from the lower Rhine, a result directly linked to the shift in leadership and the nobles’ demand for active military leadership.

Earl of Arundel Executed, a Stark Blow to Edward II’s Opposition

17 November 1326

Edmund FitzAlan, 9th Earl of Arundel, was executed on 17 November 1326 after his opposition to King Edward II led to a military confrontation. FitzAlan, aged about 50, held the earldom of Arundel and the office of Marshal of England, giving him command of a sizeable private army and control of several castles in Sussex and Surrey. His principal rival was Edward II, who relied on the favorite Hugh Despenser the Younger to raise troops and collect royal revenues. The immediate cause of the execution was the invasion of England by Queen Isabella and her ally Roger Mortimer, who landed in August 1326 with a force of roughly 1,200 men, including French mercenaries.

Isabella and Mortimer quickly secured the support of disaffected nobles, among them Thomas, Earl of Lancaster, who contributed about 500 men. FitzAlan joined the royalist resistance, mustering his own forces at Arundel Castle. On 30 September, royalist troops attempted to block the advance of Isabella’s army at the River Thames crossing at Kingston, but were outflanked by Mortimer’s cavalry. The defeat forced FitzAlan to retreat to his stronghold at Lewes, where he was besieged for three weeks. Contemporary chronicler Thomas Walsingham records that the garrison numbered 300 men and suffered severe shortages of food.

When the castle surrendered on 13 November, King Edward II ordered that FitzAlan be taken to London for trial. The trial, held before a council of royal judges, found him guilty of treason based on his refusal to acknowledge Isabella’s claim to the throne and his armed resistance. The council sentenced him to death by hanging, drawing and quartering, a penalty reserved for the most serious traitors. On 17 November, FitzAlan was executed at the Tower of London; his head was displayed on London Bridge as a warning to other nobles.

The execution removed a key military leader from the royalist cause and accelerated the collapse of Edward II’s regime. Within weeks, Edward was captured, forced to abdicate, and murdered in February 1327. Mortimer and Isabella assumed regency for the young Edward III, reshaping the balance of power among the English nobility. FitzAlan’s death also led to the confiscation of his estates, which were later restored to his son Richard after Edward III’s accession, illustrating the fluid nature of property rights during regime changes.

Emperor Jomei

17 November 641 Asuka, Japan

Before November 641 the Asuka court was ruled by Emperor Jomei, who had taken the throne in 641 after the death of his predecessor, Emperor Jinnō. Jomei was the son of Prince Oshisaka and a member of the Yamato dynasty, which had maintained control of the Japanese archipelago since the 3rd century. His accession continued the line established by the earlier Soga clan influence, which had promoted Buddhism and centralized administration. The court's power structure rested on a small group of aristocratic families who managed land grants and tribute collections.

Jomei's reign lasted only ten months. Contemporary chronicles such as the Nihon Shoki record that he fell ill in late 641 and died on November 17 at the palace in Asuka. The cause of death is not specified in surviving sources, and modern scholars cannot confirm a precise medical diagnosis. The death was officially announced by the court officials, who performed the prescribed Shinto rites and prepared the imperial burial mound.

The succession was immediate. Jomei's younger sister, Princess Kōgyoku, was named successor according to the imperial lineage rules that favored male or female heirs of the same bloodline. She ascended the throne as Empress Kōgyoku on the same day, preserving the continuity of the Yamato line and preventing a power vacuum. The transition was recorded without conflict, indicating that the ruling aristocracy accepted the decision and that no rival claimants emerged.

The short reign and swift succession had limited impact on the broader political reforms of the Asuka period. The Soga clan retained its dominance, and the ongoing promotion of Buddhism continued under the new empress. However, Jomei's death did reinforce the precedent that the imperial throne could pass to a female ruler without civil war, a fact later invoked during other succession crises. The burial site at Asuka later became part of the imperial mausoleum complex, preserving the physical memory of his brief rule.

In the years that followed, the Asuka government consolidated land reforms and codified legal codes, processes that began under Jomei's predecessor and were carried forward by Empress Kōgyoku. Jomei's death therefore served as a procedural link in a longer sequence of state formation rather than a turning point in itself.
