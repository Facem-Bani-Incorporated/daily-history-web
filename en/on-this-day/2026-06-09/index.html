Six-Day War: Israel captures the Gaza Strip

9 June 1967 Gaza Strip, Palestine

As Egyptian forces retreated from the Gaza Strip, a young Palestinian girl named Amal Abu Shanab, just 12 years old, watched in awe as Israeli tanks rolled into her neighborhood, their cannons still smoking from the intense battle that had raged on for days. The year was 1967, and the Six-Day War was nearing its end, with Israel having already captured the Sinai Peninsula and the West Bank. The Gaza Strip, a small coastal region with a population of around 350,000 Palestinians, was the last remaining stronghold of Egyptian forces in the area.

The war had begun on June 5, 1967, when Israel launched a surprise attack on Egyptian airfields, destroying nearly 90% of the country's air force. The attack was a masterstroke, giving Israel a significant advantage in the skies and allowing its ground forces to push deep into Egyptian territory. As the Israeli army advanced, it encountered fierce resistance from Egyptian troops, but ultimately, the Israelis emerged victorious, capturing the Gaza Strip on June 7, 1967.

The capture of the Gaza Strip was a pivotal moment in the war, as it gave Israel control over a strategic piece of land that bordered both Egypt and the Mediterranean Sea. The Israeli military had used a combination of air power, artillery, and infantry to overwhelm the Egyptian defenses, and by the time they entered the Gaza Strip, the Egyptians were in full retreat. As Amal Abu Shanab watched, Israeli soldiers began to take control of key installations, including the city's main hospital, where they found evidence of the intense fighting that had taken place in the preceding days.

One of the most surprising aspects of the war was the role played by Israeli military strategist, General Yitzhak Rabin, who had planned the campaign with meticulous care. Rabin, a former commander of the Israeli army's elite paratrooper unit, had used his knowledge of the Egyptian military to outmaneuver them at every turn, striking at their weak points and exploiting their mistakes. As the war drew to a close, Rabin's plan had proven to be a resounding success, with Israel having captured large swathes of territory from its Arab neighbors.

The human cost of the war, however, was significant, with over 15,000 Egyptian soldiers killed or wounded, and around 1,000 Israeli troops lost. The Gaza Strip, in particular, had been heavily bombed, with many buildings reduced to rubble and civilians caught in the crossfire. As Amal Abu Shanab navigated the ruins of her neighborhood, she saw the devastating impact of the war firsthand, with families left homeless and without access to basic necessities like food and water.

As the dust settled on the Gaza Strip, the Israeli military began to establish a new administration, with General Mordechai Gur, a seasoned officer, appointed as the region's military governor. Gur's task was to restore order and provide humanitarian aid to the local population, but it was a daunting challenge, given the scale of the destruction and the deep-seated animosity between Israelis and Palestinians. As Amal Abu Shanab looked on, she wondered what the future held for her family and her community, now under Israeli occupation.

The legacy of the Six-Day War continues to shape the Middle East to this day, with the conflict over the Gaza Strip remaining a major point of contention between Israelis and Palestinians. As Amal Abu Shanab grew older, she became a vocal advocate for Palestinian rights, using her experiences during the war to inform her activism and push for a more just and equitable solution to the conflict. And as she looked back on those fateful days in June 1967, she remembered the fear, the chaos, and the uncertainty, but also the resilience and the determination of her people, who continued to struggle for freedom and self-determination in the face of overwhelming odds.

Kosovo War: Kumanovo Agreement

9 June 1999 Kumanovo, North Macedonia

As the Yugoslav army was pulling out of Kosovo, 37,000 NATO troops were moving in, a 5,000-strong vanguard of which had already secured the border crossings within 48 hours. The speed of this operation was not just a testament to the military prowess of the alliance, but also a relief to the 850,000 refugees who had been forced to flee their homes during the 78-day bombing campaign. By the time the Kumanovo Agreement was signed on June 9, 1999, an estimated 10,000 people had lost their lives in the conflict, with many more injured or missing.

The war had begun 15 months earlier, in March 1998, when Yugoslav forces launched a crackdown on separatist rebels in Kosovo, a province with a 90% ethnic Albanian population. The conflict had escalated rapidly, with reports of massacres, rapes, and forced expulsions emerging from the region. In response, NATO had imposed economic sanctions on Yugoslavia, but these had little effect on the regime of Slobodan Milosevic, who was determined to maintain control over the province. It wasn't until the Racak massacre, in which 45 civilians were killed, that the international community decided to intervene militarily. The bombing campaign, which lasted from March 24 to June 10, 1999, involved 1,000 aircraft from 14 countries, flying 38,000 sorties and dropping 23,000 bombs.

One of the most surprising aspects of the conflict was the role played by technology. The use of drones, precision-guided munitions, and advanced communications systems allowed NATO to wage a highly effective campaign with minimal casualties – only 2 NATO soldiers were killed in action, and both of these deaths were the result of friendly fire. Meanwhile, the Yugoslav army, which had 114,000 troops in Kosovo at the height of the conflict, was able to inflict significant damage on the civilian population, but was ultimately unable to withstand the might of the NATO alliance. As General Wesley Clark, the Supreme Allied Commander Europe, said at the time, 'We're not just fighting an army, we're fighting a regime, and we're fighting a strategy that is based on violence and intimidation.'

The Kumanovo Agreement, which was signed in the presence of General Clark and the Yugoslav Deputy Prime Minister, marked the beginning of the end of the conflict. Under the terms of the agreement, Yugoslav forces were to withdraw from Kosovo within 11 days, and NATO troops were to enter the province to maintain order and protect the civilian population. The agreement also established the United Nations Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo, which would oversee the administration of the province until a permanent solution could be found. As the Yugoslav army pulled out, 5,000 Russian troops, who had been stationed in Bosnia, moved into Kosovo, causing a brief standoff with NATO forces. However, this was eventually resolved, and the province began the long process of rebuilding and recovery.

In the years that followed, Kosovo would face many challenges, from rebuilding its infrastructure to establishing a functioning government. But on that day in June 1999, as the Kumanovo Agreement was signed, there was a sense of hope and relief. The war was over, and the people of Kosovo could begin to rebuild their lives. As one refugee, who had been forced to flee her home in Pristina, said, 'We're just glad it's over. We can finally go home, and start to rebuild our lives.' The agreement had taken 16 hours of negotiation to reach, but in the end, it had brought peace to a region that had known only war for 15 months. And as the 1,000-page agreement was being signed, 20,000 Kosovo Albanians were already on their way back to their homes, a journey of 100 miles that would take them 10 hours to complete.
