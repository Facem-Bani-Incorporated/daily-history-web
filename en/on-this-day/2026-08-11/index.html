Great Famine Grips Europe

11 August 1315

A king, stranded. Not by enemy forces, nor by a treacherous advisor, but by the utter, bewildering absence of bread. It’s August 11, 1315, and Edward II, King of England, finds himself in a predicament that would be farcical if it weren't so desperately grim. His royal retinue, a sprawling organism of several hundred mouths, moves slowly across the sodden landscape of England, their usual provisioning system having collapsed like a waterlogged roof.

Today, the royal household is near St Albans, a prosperous town in better times. The King’s harbinger, a man whose job is usually to command, not plead, approaches the local baker. He brandishes the royal warrant, a document that, for centuries, has guaranteed the king first pick of all available provisions, often at fixed prices. He demands bread. The baker, however, merely shrugs, perhaps offers a weary, apologetic half-bow. There is no bread. Not for the King, not for his nobles, not for the common folk whose faces are already beginning to hollow from hunger. The baker’s grain stores are empty, his ovens cold. The royal purse, usually a magic wand, is now just a heavy leather bag of coins that no one wants in exchange for what isn't there.

This isn't just a local anomaly. This is the Great Famine, now a full year into its relentless grip, and it has already begun to reshape the very fabric of European life. It began with the rains, an unceasing, biblical deluge that started in the spring of 1315. Week after week, month after month, the heavens wept. Fields became bogs, seeds rotted in the ground, and what little grain managed to sprout was often washed away or mildewed before harvest. The summer of 1315 was a sodden, cheerless affair, and by August, the harvest, typically a time of plenty, was a cruel joke.

Suddenly, the usual rhythm of medieval life—feast and fast, sowing and reaping—was thrown into disarray. The price of wheat, which had been around 5 shillings a quarter in 1314, skyrocketed to 40 shillings by mid-1315, an eight-fold increase. Even if the King had the coin, the sheer logistics of feeding his massive household became impossible. His purveyors, accustomed to simply requisitioning supplies, now faced empty markets and hostile glares. They couldn't conjure food out of thin air, and the people who *did* have small stores were hoarding them, clinging to life with every last grain.

This wasn't just a temporary inconvenience. The Great Famine would last, in its most acute phase, for two years, and its aftershocks for several more. It would claim millions of lives across northern Europe, perhaps 10-15% of the population in some regions. People resorted to eating cats, dogs, and even, in desperate whispers, their fellow humans. The famine would lead to widespread disease, social unrest, and a profound psychological trauma that would linger for generations, effectively resetting the demographic clock of the continent.

Edward II, who had a reputation for being rather less kingly than his imposing father, was not alone in his hunger. Even the most powerful man in England was subject to the whims of the weather, a stark reminder that even a crown offered no immunity from the most basic of human needs. His inability to secure a loaf of bread, a simple, fundamental staple, underscores the sheer, terrifying scale of a catastrophe that stripped away the thin veneer of civilization and reduced kings to the same desperate straits as their lowliest subjects. The rain, it seemed, was truly an equal-opportunity destroyer.

Theodoric Defeats Odoacer at Battle of Adda

11 August 490 Milan, Italy

For fourteen years, Odoacer had been a ghost in the imperial machine, ruling Italy since 476 CE without a Roman emperor to boss him around. He was a German chieftain who wore the purple, a mercenary who had dispatched the last Western Roman Emperor, a 16-year-old boy named Romulus Augustulus, with little more than a pension and a shrug. Odoacer, at the head of perhaps 20,000 men, had carved out a functional if fragile kingdom. Then came Theodoric, an Ostrogothic king, a man who had spent ten years as a hostage in Constantinople, learning the delicate dance of power. He arrived not alone, but with an entire nation of perhaps 250,000 people, their wagons stretching for miles across the plains, seeking a new home. This was not an invasion; it was a migration with blades.

Their journey was an epic undertaking, a 2,000-kilometer trek from Moesia, over the Alps, into Italy. Theodoric had already bloodied Odoacer twice, first at the Isonzo River in August 489, then decisively at Verona a month later, where Odoacer lost a staggering 10,000 men. Now, on August 11, 490, the two men faced each other again on the banks of the Adda River, east of Milan. The air hung heavy with the late summer heat, the scent of damp earth rising from the river, and the metallic tang of fear from men who had already tasted defeat.

Odoacer’s forces, though diminished, still numbered in the tens of thousands, dug in behind the flowing brown water of the Adda. Theodoric, ever the tactician, was not alone this time. His ally, Alaric II, King of the Visigoths, had brought fresh cavalry. Imagine the thunder of perhaps 5,000 Visigothic horsemen, their war cries echoing across the water, hitting Odoacer’s entrenched lines. The numerical advantage, which had shifted throughout the campaign, was now decisively with Theodoric. The battle was a brutal, grinding affair, lasting for hours, the riverbanks slick with mud and blood. Odoacer’s once-loyal commanders, sensing the tide turning, began to desert him, their defection a fatal wound to his army’s morale.

The defeat was absolute. Odoacer’s remaining forces, perhaps 15,000 men, were scattered or captured. He fled, leading a desperate retreat to Ravenna, the formidable Adriatic city that had served as the Western Roman Empire’s capital for nearly a century. Ravenna, with its marshy defenses and stout walls, was a fortress designed to withstand such onslaughts. Odoacer believed he could hold out indefinitely, perhaps for years, against Theodoric’s besieging army. He had stocked it with enough provisions to last three long years.

Indeed, the siege dragged on, an interminable stalemate that stretched from 490 to 493 CE. Theodoric, unable to break the city, finally resorted to diplomacy. A deal was struck: they would co-rule Italy. On February 25, 493, Theodoric entered Ravenna. Just ten days later, on March 5, during a supposed banquet of reconciliation in the imperial palace, Theodoric drew his sword. With his own hand, he cleaved Odoacer in two. “Where is God?” Odoacer is said to have cried out, his last words before the blade struck. Theodoric, reportedly shocked by the man’s height, remarked, “This poor wretch must have had no bones in his body.” With that single, shocking act of betrayal, Theodoric the Great cemented his rule, ushering in 33 years of Ostrogothic dominance, built on the bones of a promise. He had just didn’t want to share.
