Panama Breaks Away from Colombia with U.S. Backing

3 November 1903 Panama

Panama's separation from Colombia in 1903 created a sovereign state that allowed the United States to build a trans‑isthmus canal. The United States had negotiated a $10 million payment to Colombia in the 1901 Hay, Herrán Treaty, but Colombia's Senate rejected it on 20 May 1903, leaving the U.S. without legal rights to the canal zone.

The decisive mechanism was a covert diplomatic and military operation led by U.S. Secretary of State John Hay and the newly appointed U.S. Minister to Colombia, William R. C. Miller. Hay authorized a $40 million loan to the future Panamanian government, which financed a small force of 1,000 U.S. Marines stationed on the Caribbean side of the isthmus. Their presence prevented Colombian troops, numbering about 2,000, from moving to suppress the revolt.

On 3 November 1903 a provisional government was declared in the city of Panama by a junta of three local leaders: José Agustín Arango, Federico Barreda, and Carlos Antonio López. The junta immediately requested U.S. recognition, which was granted within 24 hours. The United States recognized Panama at 7:00 a.m. on 4 November, and the same day signed the Hay‑Brown Treaty, securing a 99‑year lease of a 10‑mile wide canal zone for $10 million upfront and an annual payment of $250,000.

The new Panamanian constitution, adopted on 20 February 1904, defined a presidential system with a unicameral legislature of 30 members. The first president, Manuel Amador Terrero, was elected by the legislature on 15 October 1904 with 26 of 30 votes, reflecting the influence of the United States, which supplied 75 percent of Panama's export revenue in 1905, primarily from canal construction contracts.

The immediate consequence was the start of construction on the Panama Canal on 4 May 1904, a project that would employ up to 40,000 workers at its peak and cost an estimated $375 million (equivalent to about $10 billion in 2020 dollars). The canal opened on 15 August 1914, shortening maritime routes by roughly 8,000 nautical miles between New York and San Francisco and establishing Panama as a strategic geopolitical corridor.

Kiel Mutiny Sparks German Revolution

3 November 1918 Kiel, Germany

The immediate trigger of the German Revolution was the decision by the German high command on 24 October 1918 to order the fleet at Kiel to sail for a final engagement with the British Royal Navy. The order was issued despite the armistice negotiations and the obvious lack of fuel and morale, and it was intended to demonstrate that Germany could still fight. In reality the order exposed the collapse of discipline: on 3 November the sailors of the torpedo boats and the larger warships refused to obey, seized the naval base and raised red flags.

The refusal was not a spontaneous outburst of anger but the result of months of war fatigue, food shortages and the spread of socialist pamphlets among the crews. By early November the Kiel workers’ council, formed on 4 November, had already coordinated with the mutineers, providing the organizational backbone for the uprising. The council elected a chairman, Karl Artelt, who negotiated with the local commander, Admiral von Tirpitz, demanding the release of imprisoned comrades. The admiral’s refusal led to the arrest of several officers and the establishment of a sailors’ committee that took control of the ships.

Popular accounts often portray the Kiel mutiny as a purely naval affair that sparked a nationwide revolution. The record shows that the mutiny was closely linked to the broader political crisis: the Social Democratic Party had already withdrawn its support from Chancellor Prince Max von Baden on 9 November, and the emperor had already signed the abdication on 9 November, two days after the mutiny began. The revolution therefore hinged on the loss of authority of both the military and the monarchy, not merely on a single port revolt.

Within three days the sailors’ council sent delegations to Berlin, where they met with workers from the Spartacus League and the Independent Social Democratic Party. The councils demanded an end to the war, the release of political prisoners and the formation of a democratic republic. The provisional government, formed on 9 November, granted these demands and announced the establishment of the German Republic. The Kiel uprising thus served as the catalyst that transformed existing discontent into a coordinated political movement, leading to the proclamation of the Weimar Republic and the eventual signing of the Treaty of Versailles on 28 June 1919.

The concrete consequence of the Kiel mutiny was the abolition of the imperial throne and the creation of a parliamentary system, which reshaped German governance for the next two decades.

Henry VIII Declares Himself Head of Church

3 November 1534 England, United Kingdom

The 1534 Act of Supremacy declared King Henry VIII the supreme head of the Church of England, replacing the Pope as religious authority. The law was drafted to resolve a conflict between Henry and Pope Clement VII over the annulment of Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon. Henry needed a male heir; the Pope refused the annulment, and the English clergy could not grant it. By transferring ecclesiastical authority to the monarch, Henry created a legal basis for a separate English church.

Parliament passed the act after a series of petitions and a brief period of debate. The final text required every clergy member to acknowledge the king’s supremacy within six months, under penalty of treason. Henry personally signed the document and ordered the clergy to take the oath. The act also mandated the seizure of church lands that had previously been under papal control, adding a financial incentive for the Crown.

The immediate effect was the removal of papal influence from English governance. Approximately 2,000 senior clergy were forced to swear allegiance; about 200 refused and were imprisoned or executed, including Sir Thomas More, who was executed in 1535. The redistribution of monastic property transferred roughly 300,000 acres of land to the Crown and to nobles who supported the reform, enriching the royal treasury by an estimated £200,000 per year, a substantial increase in state revenue.

Long‑term consequences reshaped English society. The new hierarchy placed the monarch at the head of religious doctrine, allowing subsequent monarchs to alter doctrine without external approval. This legal precedent paved the way for further reforms under Edward VI and the brief restoration of Catholicism under Mary I, before Elizabeth I solidified a moderate Anglican settlement. The act also sparked resistance in regions such as the north of England, where Catholic sentiment remained strong, leading to the Pilgrimage of Grace in 1536, a rebellion involving an estimated 30,000 participants that was eventually suppressed.

By establishing a national church under royal control, the act altered the relationship between religion and state for the roughly four million inhabitants of England at the time. It set a template for state‑controlled religion that influenced later British colonies and contributed to the development of religious tolerance debates in the 17th century. The legal framework created in 1534 remains the foundation of the Church of England today.

Fashoda Crisis Ends as France Pulls Troops

3 November 1898 Fashoda, Sudan (now South Sudan)

France withdrew its expeditionary force from Fashoda on 3 November 1898, ending a confrontation with Britain over control of the Nile valley. The crisis began in 1898 when French explorer Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza led a column to the Sudanese outpost of Fashoda, intending to claim a route to the Indian Ocean that would rival British influence. At the same time, a British force under Lord Kitchener arrived to secure the same location as part of the Anglo‑Egyptian campaign against the Mahdist state. Both sides claimed sovereignty, and each sent troops to the remote settlement, creating a standoff that threatened war between two great powers.

The underlying mechanism was imperial rivalry over the Nile basin, a region whose navigation could determine the economic fate of Egypt and the colonies beyond. France sought an east‑west corridor from West Africa to the Red Sea, while Britain aimed to protect the north‑south flow of the Nile to its Egyptian holdings. Control of Fashoda would have given France a foothold that could block British river traffic, a prospect that British officials deemed unacceptable. Consequently, diplomatic negotiations in Paris and London focused on balancing the strategic value of the outpost against the risk of a European war.

Negotiations were led by French Prime Minister Pierre‑Waldeck-Rousseau and British Foreign Secretary Lord Lansdowne. On 13 October 1898, Waldeck‑Rousseau sent a note stating that France would not contest British authority in Sudan, while Lansdowne replied that France could retain commercial privileges in the region. The French command, under Major Marchand, received orders to evacuate on 30 October and completed the withdrawal on 3 November. No shots were fired during the retreat, and the French troops left the fort with their weapons intact.

The withdrawal removed the immediate cause of a possible Anglo‑French war and paved the way for the Entente Cordiale signed in 1904. By accepting British dominance in the Nile valley, France redirected its colonial focus to West Africa, where it expanded its territories without direct competition from Britain. The episode also demonstrated that diplomatic pressure, rather than military confrontation, could resolve overlapping imperial claims, a practice that later shaped the handling of colonial disputes in the early twentieth century.

The concrete consequence of the Fashoda withdrawal was the establishment of a clear sphere of influence: Britain secured the Nile corridor, and France concentrated on its West African empire. This division of African territories persisted until the decolonisation movements of the mid‑twentieth century.

Napoleon’s Grande Armée Crushed at Vyazma

3 November 1812 Vyazma, Russia

The Grande Armée entered Vyazma on 2 November 1812 with roughly 25,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry and 150 artillery pieces, still organized in three corps under Marshal Ney, Marshal Davout and General Oudinot. Their objective was to cross the Smolensk road and keep the supply line open for the retreating main army, which had been forced westward after the loss at Borodino.

Russian forces, commanded by General Barclay de Tolly and General Wittgenstein, had assembled about 30,000 troops, including 10,000 regulars of the 2nd and 3rd Infantry Divisions and 20,000 militia from the surrounding provinces. They occupied the western outskirts of Vyazma, constructing earthworks and positioning cannons to command the main road and the two bridges over the Vyazma River.

At dawn on 3 November, the French launched a frontal assault on the Russian redoubts. Ney ordered his infantry to advance in line formation, supported by cavalry charges intended to outflank the militia positions. The Russian artillery opened fire at 800 meters, inflicting heavy casualties on the leading French battalions. French reports record 3,200 killed or wounded in the first two hours, while Russian losses were about 2,500, according to the official dispatch from Barclay de Tolly.

The decisive factor was the failure of French supply wagons to reach the battlefield. A Russian cavalry raid on the supply column on 2 November had captured 1,200 wagons, depriving the French of food, ammunition and horse fodder. Without resupply, French artillery could not maintain a sustained barrage, and the infantry exhausted their ammunition after three volleys. Marshal Davout, noting the shortage, ordered a withdrawal at 14:00, pulling his corps back toward the western road.

By nightfall the French had abandoned Vyazma, leaving behind 1,500 dead and 2,000 prisoners, including several senior officers. The Russians reclaimed the bridges and repaired the road, allowing their own retreating forces to move westward unimpeded. The loss of Vyazma removed the last significant French foothold on the Smolensk road, forcing Napoleon to order a full retreat toward the Berezina River.

The defeat at Vyazma accelerated the disintegration of the Grande Armée. Within two weeks the army's strength fell from about 80,000 to fewer than 30,000 due to desertion, disease and continued Russian attacks. The retreat from Moscow, already in crisis, became a catastrophic loss of manpower and materiel, contributing directly to Napoleon's eventual abdication in 1814.
