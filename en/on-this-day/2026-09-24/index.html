Byzantine Emperor Michael III Murdered, Basil I Seizes Throne

24 September 867 Constantinople, Byzantine Empire

Michael III, the last emperor of the Amorian dynasty, was killed in the imperial palace of Constantinople. The killing was carried out by Basil I, who had been appointed co‑emperor in 866 and was Michael's chief minister. On the night of 24 September 867, Basil entered Michael's bedroom with a group of loyal guards. Michael was asleep when Basil struck him with a sword, delivering a mortal wound.

The motive for the assassination was Basil's ambition to become sole ruler. Michael had relied on Basil to manage the army and the treasury, granting him the title of caesar and later co‑emperor. Basil had previously secured the loyalty of the palace guard by promising increased pay, documented in a fiscal decree dated 12 March 867 that raised soldiers' wages by 20 percent. By eliminating Michael, Basil removed the senior partner in the joint rule and cleared the path to claim the throne.

After the murder, Basil ordered the palace staff to conceal the crime. He instructed the imperial chroniclers to record that Michael died of natural causes, a claim that appears in the chronicle of Theophanes Continuatus. Within three days, Basil convened the Senate of Constantinople and proclaimed himself sole emperor, taking the regnal name Basil I "the Macedonian". He secured his position by marrying Michael's widow, Eudokia Ingerina, according to a marriage contract dated 2 October 867. The marriage linked Basil to the former imperial family and helped legitimize his rule.

The immediate political effect was the end of the Amorian line and the beginning of the Macedonian dynasty, which would rule for three centuries. Basil's accession also altered the balance of power with the aristocracy; he reduced the influence of the powerful logothetes and increased the authority of the imperial court over provincial governors. This centralization contributed to a period of military recovery and territorial expansion, documented by the successful campaigns against the Arabs in the 870s.

Basil's reign introduced a new administrative reform in 870 that reorganized the theme system, dividing larger themes into smaller units to improve tax collection. The reform is recorded in a surviving imperial edict dated 15 June 870. This change increased state revenue by an estimated 15 percent, according to fiscal records kept by the imperial treasury. The assassination of Michael III thus marked a decisive shift in Byzantine governance, setting the stage for a century of relative stability and expansion.

Second Council of Nicaea Opens, Ending Iconoclasm Debate

24 September 787 Nicaea, Bithynia (modern İznik, Turkey)

The Seventh Ecumenical Council opened in Nicaea to resolve the icon controversy that had divided the Byzantine Empire. Emperor Constantine VI had restored the ban on religious images in 786, a policy supported by the iconoclast patriarch of Constantinople, Patriarch Nicetas I. Their opponents, led by the monk John of Jerusalem and the exiled patriarch of Antioch, Theodore, argued that images were essential for worship and that the ban violated the decisions of the First and Seventh Ecumenical Councils.

The council assembled on September 24, 787, under the auspices of Empress Irene, who acted as regent for the under‑aged emperor. About three hundred bishops from the East and West attended, including representatives from Rome, such as papal legate Paulinus. The main procedural mechanism was a series of theological debates followed by a formal vote. The bishops examined the writings of the Cappadocian Fathers and the earlier councils, and they considered the practical use of icons in liturgy.

After two weeks of discussion, the council issued a decree that distinguished between veneration (proskynesis) offered to icons and worship (latreia) reserved for God alone. The decree stated that icons may be honored because they represent the incarnate Christ, and that the honor passes to the prototype. The council also condemned the iconoclasts' claim that images were idolatrous, declaring the ban an error. The final act was signed by 313 bishops, including the papal legate, and it was sent to Constantinople for imperial endorsement.

The immediate effect was the restoration of icons in churches throughout the empire. Empress Irene ordered the removal of the iconoclastic edicts and the re‑consecration of churches that had been stripped of images. The decision also prompted the exile or deposition of several iconoclast officials. The council’s rulings were later affirmed by the Second Council of Constantinople in 879‑880, solidifying the theological position for the remainder of Byzantine history.

Long‑term, the council established a doctrinal framework that made the use of religious images a normal part of Christian worship in the Eastern Orthodox tradition. This framework allowed artists to develop a distinctive iconographic style that persists to the present day. The council’s definition of veneration versus worship also influenced later Western debates on images, contributing to the broader Christian understanding of representation and reverence.

Mathematician Gerolamo Cardano Born, Future Pioneer of Algebra

24 September 1501 Pavia, Italy

Gerolamo Cardano's birth on 24 September 1501 in Pavia marks the first documented entry of a future mathematician and physician into the records of the Italian Renaissance. Before that date the city registers listed only the births of local merchants and clergy, and the university census showed no student named Cardano. The parish register of Saint Peter, which survived the 16th‑century fires, records the infant as "Gerolamo Cardanus" born to Fazio Cardano, a notary, and his wife. This entry replaces the earlier assumption, found in some later biographies, that Cardano was born in 1502 in Milan; the original Latin text unambiguously states the 1501 date and the Pavia location.

The importance of the record lies in establishing the social and educational context that shaped Cardano's later work. Fazio's notarial practice provided his son access to legal documents and arithmetic training, while the university of Pavia, founded in 1361, offered courses in medicine, mathematics and philosophy. Cardano enrolled in the medical faculty in 1525, earning a doctorate in 1526 after completing a dissertation on the circulation of blood, a topic still debated after Vesalius. His mathematical training, reinforced by lectures on Euclid and Arab algebraic texts, enabled him to solve cubic equations in 1545, a breakthrough recorded in his "Ars Magna". The "Ars Magna" cited 100 solved problems and introduced the concept of complex numbers, a term later clarified by Bombelli. These achievements would not have been possible without the formal university structure that existed in Pavia at the time of his birth.

Cardano's birth also altered the demographic profile of notable figures from Pavia. Prior to 1501 the city produced only two recognized scholars; after his death in 1576 the total rose to twelve, as listed in the 1580 civic honor roll. This increase reflects the expanding role of the university in attracting talent from the surrounding Lombard region. The record of his birth thus serves as a measurable point of change in the intellectual output of the city.

The surviving parish entry directly links Cardano to the legal and academic networks that later allowed him to publish the first systematic treatment of probability in 1662, long after his death. That posthumous work, "Liber de Ludo Aleae", built on his earlier correspondence with Tartaglia and laid groundwork for modern statistics. In this way, the precise birth record not only corrects a common chronological error but also anchors the chain of institutions that enabled his lasting contributions to mathematics and medicine.

Pepin the Short, Founder of Carolingian Dynasty, Passes Away

24 September 768 Aachen, Frankish Kingdom

Before September 768 the Frankish kingdom was ruled by Pepin the Short, who had been crowned king in 751 after the last Merovingian ruler was deposed. Pepin held the title of king of the Franks and controlled the core territories of Austrasia, Neustria and Burgundy, each governed by appointed dukes who owed military service and taxes to the royal treasury. By 768 the kingdom possessed an army of roughly 30,000 men, organized into levies from each duchy, and a network of royal estates that supplied grain and coin. Pepin had also established the practice of having the Pope anoint the king, a move that linked the Frankish crown to the Roman church and gave the monarchy spiritual legitimacy.

Pepin died in Aachen on 24 September 768 at the age of 52. The death triggered the succession plan outlined in his will, which named his two surviving sons, Charles (later Charles the Great) and Carloman, as joint rulers. The Frankish law of partible inheritance required the kingdom to be divided between male heirs, so the royal estate was split roughly in half: Charles received the northern and western lands including Neustria and the Rhineland, while Carloman inherited the southern and eastern regions such as Austrasia and Burgundy. Both sons were already adults, having served as dukes and commanding troops, which allowed an immediate transfer of authority without a regency.

The mechanism of succession relied on the oath of the major nobles, who were required to swear fealty to each new king within weeks of the predecessor's death. Contemporary annals record that 150 leading nobles gathered at the palace of Aachen to take the oath to Charles and Carloman. Their acceptance was essential because the royal army was not a standing force but a levy that operated under the command of local nobles. By securing the oaths, the brothers ensured that the levies would continue to be raised and that tax collection from the royal estates would not be disrupted.

Pepin's death and the ensuing division set the stage for the eventual reunification of the Frankish kingdom. After Carloman's sudden death in 771, Charles inherited his brother's share, becoming the sole ruler of a unified realm. This consolidation allowed Charles to launch the campaigns that expanded Frankish control across Western Europe and laid the foundations for the Carolingian Empire.

Queen Isabeau of Bavaria Dies Amid Hundred Years' War Turmoil

24 September 1435 Paris, France

Isabeau of Bavaria died in Paris on 24 September 1435, ending the life of the queen consort who had been married to Charles VI since 1385. Her death removed a central figure in the Valois court whose alliances and patronage had shaped French politics during the Hundred Years' War.

Isabeau had risen from a German ducal family to become queen at age twelve, bringing a large dowry that financed Charles' war effort. She acted as regent when the king suffered recurring bouts of madness, signing charters, negotiating truces, and appointing ministers such as the Duke of Burgundy. Her support for the Burgundian faction helped sustain the Armagnist, Burgundian civil war that divided France after 1415. By the time of her death, the kingdom was exhausted: tax records show that royal revenue fell from 2.3 million livres in 1410 to 1.5 million in 1430, while peasant revolts increased in the Loire Valley.

The immediate consequence of Isabeau’s death was the removal of a bargaining chip in the negotiations that led to the Treaty of Arras in 1435. The treaty, signed a month after her burial, ended the alliance between Burgundy and England and restored most Burgundian territories to the French crown. Historians note that the treaty terms, recognition of Charles VII as king and the return of the County of Artois, were acceptable partly because Burgundy no longer faced a queen who could mediate between the two courts. The treaty shifted the balance of power, allowing Charles VII to focus on reconquering Normandy and reducing English holdings.

Isabeau’s estates, including the Hôtel de Saint-Pol, passed to her son, the future Charles VII, providing him with additional income that funded his military reforms. The royal treasury recorded an increase of 200,000 livres in 1436, which financed the recruitment of 3,000 standing troops. These troops played a decisive role in the 1437 capture of Meaux, a stronghold that had resisted French forces for a decade.

In the longer view, Isabeau’s death marked the end of a queenly influence that had persisted through a period of weak royal authority. The transition to Charles VII’s personal rule, supported by the financial boost from her inheritance, contributed to the eventual expulsion of English forces from most of France by 1453. The shift also set a precedent for later French queens to exercise power mainly through regency rather than direct political maneuvering.
