James VI Marries Anne of Denmark, Paving Way for the British Crown Union

23 November 1589 Oslo, Norway

The diplomatic decision that enabled James VI of Scotland to marry Anne of Denmark was made in January 1588, when the Scottish Privy Council authorized a marriage contract that required a dowry of 100,000 dalers and a pledge of mutual defence against England. Popular accounts often say the marriage was a simple love match, but the surviving letters between James and the Danish envoy Breide Rantzau show that the union was primarily a strategic move to secure a Protestant alliance and to strengthen James’s claim to the English throne.

James sent a delegation to Copenhagen in June 1588, but the fleet was delayed by a storm in the North Sea and forced to seek shelter in Oslo. The Norwegian capital became the unexpected venue for the wedding because the Danish crown prince, Christian, was stationed there with his troops. On 23 November 1589, James and Anne were married in the Oslo Cathedral before an audience of 150 officials, including the Norwegian governor Axel Gyldenstierne. The ceremony followed the Lutheran rite prescribed by the Danish church, and the marriage contract was witnessed by representatives of both kingdoms.

The contract stipulated that any children of the marriage would inherit both the Scottish and Danish thrones, and that Scotland would provide 5,000 dalers annually for the upkeep of Anne’s household. It also required James to send a fleet of 20 warships to protect Danish merchant routes in the Baltic, a clause that was never fully implemented because of James’s limited naval resources. Contemporary records from the English ambassador in Oslo note that the English government viewed the alliance with suspicion, fearing it could shift the balance of power in the North Sea.

The marriage produced three surviving children, the eldest being Henry Frederick, Prince of Wales, who died in 1612, and the second, Charles, who became Charles I of England. Most importantly, the union gave James a legitimate claim to the English crown through his marriage to a princess of a kingdom allied with England’s Protestant adversaries. When Elizabeth I died in 1603, James succeeded without armed conflict, becoming James I of England and uniting the crowns of Scotland and England. The Oslo wedding therefore acted as the diplomatic foundation for the eventual creation of the British monarchy.

Thespis Takes the Stage: Birth of Western Acting

23 November 535

Thespis of Icaria is recorded as the first person to step onto a Greek stage and speak as a fictional character. Before his performance, religious festivals in ancient Greece featured choral hymns and a priestly recitation of myth, but no individual assumed a role distinct from the narrator. The innovation occurred during the festival of Dionysus in Athens, where Thespis introduced a second voice that opposed the chorus, creating a dialogue that distinguished actor from narrator.

The mechanism of this change was the addition of a separate speaking part that represented a character rather than a deity or communal voice. By assigning a mask, costume, and a scripted line to a single performer, the drama could present conflict and personality on stage. This required the audience to recognize that the spoken words came from a person playing a role, not from the chorus itself. The practice also demanded that the actor memorize lines and respond to the chorus’s chants, establishing a new theatrical convention.

Ancient sources such as Aristotle’s *Poetics* attribute the invention of drama to Thespis, though the historical record is sparse. The only contemporary evidence is a later inscription that lists Thespis among early poets, and later writers credit him with the word "thespian" for actors. No exact script survives, but the structure of later Greek tragedies shows the lasting impact of his method.

The immediate effect was that subsequent festivals regularly featured a single actor, then two, then three, expanding the possibilities of plot and character. By the fifth century BCE playwrights like Aeschylus and Sophocles built entire tragedies on the dialogue between actors and chorus, a form that became the foundation of Western theatre. The shift also altered social perception of performance; acting moved from a ritual function to a professional art, eventually creating a class of citizens who earned a living by interpreting characters.

Thespis’s contribution opened a channel for storytelling that could explore human motives, moral dilemmas, and political satire. This capacity to dramatize individual experience spread through the Hellenistic world, influencing Roman theatre and later European stages. The concept of a person portraying another person on a scripted platform remains a core element of modern drama, film, and television, making Thespis’s 534 BC innovation a pivotal moment in cultural history.

Ferdinand III Captures Seville, Turning Point in the Reconquista

23 November 1248 Seville, Spain

Christian forces under King Ferdinand III seized Seville after a siege that lasted more than two years. The siege relied on a systematic blockade: engineers built a series of siege towers, battering rams, and a circumvallation wall to cut off supplies and prevent any relief army from entering the city. Inside, the Muslim defenders, led by Governor Al-Muzaffar, attempted intermittent sallies but could not break the encirclement because the Christian navy controlled the Guadalquivir River, denying the city access to external trade.

Ferdinand III commanded an army of roughly 30,000 men, composed of Castilian knights, infantry, and mercenaries from Aragon and Portugal. The defenders numbered about 12,000, including local militia and a contingent of North African troops. The decisive assault on 23 November 1248 involved three coordinated attacks: a frontal push by heavy cavalry, a breach created by engineers using siege engines, and a simultaneous assault on the western gate by infantry. Casualty figures are uncertain, but contemporary chronicles record approximately 1,200 Christian deaths and at least 2,000 Muslim casualties, including many non‑combatants who perished when the city walls collapsed.

The capture of Seville shifted the balance of power in the Iberian Peninsula. Seville was the second largest city in al‑Andalus, with an estimated population of 80,000, and its loss deprived the Muslim kingdom of a major economic and cultural centre. Ferdinand redistributed the city's lands to his nobles, granting them tax‑exempt estates that increased Castilian revenue by an estimated 15 percent. The Christian administration introduced the fuero of Seville, a municipal charter that imposed Castilian law, reorganized the tax system, and encouraged settlement by Christian colonists. Within five years, the Christian population of the city grew to 20,000, while many Muslims were either expelled or forced into a tributary status known as the mudéjar community.

The fall of Seville also had long‑term strategic effects. It opened the route for further conquests toward the southern coast, leading to the capture of Cádiz in 1262 and the eventual completion of the Reconquista in 1492. The siege demonstrated the effectiveness of combined land‑sea operations and the use of engineered siege works, influencing later Castilian campaigns in Granada. The redistribution of wealth and land after the siege contributed to the rise of a powerful Castilian aristocracy, which later financed the voyages of Columbus and the expansion of the Spanish empire.

Battle of Chattanooga Begins: Union Breaks Confederate Siege

23 November 1863 Chattanooga, Tennessee, USA

In the autumn of 1863 the Confederate Army of Tennessee held Chattanooga, a rail hub that linked the Deep South with the Midwest. General Braxton Bragg commanded about 45,000 men entrenched on the surrounding heights, while Union forces under Major General Ulysses S Grant numbered roughly 70,000 and were positioned in three separate corps to the north, east and west of the city. Since September the Confederates had confined the Union supply line to the narrow “Cracker Line” and had threatened to starve the Union army into withdrawal.

Grant ordered a coordinated assault to break the siege. The plan called for Major General William T Sherman’s troops to cross the Tennessee River at Brown's Ferry, securing a supply route and threatening Bragg’s right flank. Simultaneously Major General George H Thomas was to attack the Confederate positions on Missionary Ridge, while Major General Joseph Hooker advanced from the north to seize Lookout Mountain. Sherman’s men completed the ferry crossing on November 23 and established a bridgehead, enabling a flow of provisions that relieved the Union’s logistical strain.

On November 24 Thomas launched a frontal assault on the heavily fortified ridge. The Union infantry advanced in tight lines, suffered initial repulse, but a sudden collapse of the Confederate left flank allowed the Union troops to roll down the slope. Sherman’s forces seized the adjacent heights, and Hooker’s troops secured Lookout Mountain after a brief engagement. Bragg’s army withdrew from the field, leaving approximately 3,000 dead or wounded on each side, while Union casualties numbered about 5,800. The loss of Chattanooga opened the gateway for the Union to advance into Georgia.

The victory forced Bragg to retreat to Dalton, Georgia, and gave Grant control of the vital rail junction. This control enabled the Union to launch the Atlanta Campaign later in 1864, which contributed directly to President Abraham Lincoln’s re‑election. The battle also elevated Thomas’s reputation, earning him the nickname “The Rock of Chickamauga.” The Confederate defeat at Chattanooga marked the end of major offensive operations in the western theater and shifted strategic initiative permanently to the Union.

St. John Slave Revolt Ignites One of the Longest Uprisings in the Americas

23 November 1733 St. John, Danish West Indies

In November 1733 enslaved laborers on the Danish West Indies island of St. John organized the largest slave uprising recorded in the Caribbean. The revolt began after a group of about 150 men, women and children seized the plantation of a Danish merchant named Frederik von Scholten at 2 a.m. on November 23. They locked the overseer inside the main house, seized firearms stored for the militia, and raised a makeshift flag made from a torn sail. The insurgents then marched to the nearby settlement of Coral Bay, where they released about 200 additional slaves from nearby sugar works.

The rebels established a council led by a man identified in colonial records as a former African captain named Breve. The council issued a written proclamation demanding freedom, the return of families separated by sale, and the end of the Danish Crown's tax on slave labor. The document, dated November 24, ordered the destruction of all plantation houses that refused to join the movement. About 500 enslaved people responded to the call, bringing the total number of participants to roughly 650.

Colonial authorities in the nearby island of St. Thomas learned of the rebellion on November 25 and dispatched a force of 120 Danish soldiers under the command of Governor-General Christian Suhm. The troops arrived on St. John on December 1, landing at Coral Bay with two cannons and a supply of muskets. They encountered organized resistance at a fortified position on a hill called Little Hill, where the rebels had erected earthworks. During the ensuing battle the Danish forces suffered eight killed and twelve wounded, while rebel casualties were recorded as 30 dead and 45 wounded.

On December 5 the Danish troops launched a coordinated assault using the cannons to breach the earthworks. The rebels, outgunned and lacking ammunition, began to surrender in small groups. By December 9 the last holdout was captured, and Governor Suhm ordered the execution of 23 leaders, including Breve, by hanging. The remaining participants were sold to other islands, and the Danish administration introduced stricter controls, including a decree on December 15 that required every plantation to keep a permanent armed guard.

The 1733 insurrection lasted six months, making it one of the longest slave revolts in the Americas. Its suppression led to a harsher legal regime, but the rebellion also forced the Danish West India Company to reconsider the cost of maintaining large slave populations, influencing later debates about emancipation in the Danish colonies.
