Florence Nightingale pioneers modern nursing at the Crimean War

21 October 1854 Crimean Peninsula

Florence Nightingale arrived at Scutari on October 21 1854 with a contingent of 38 trained nurses, the first organized nursing unit attached to a British army hospital. The British Army field hospital had previously relied on untrained orderlies and local civilians, resulting in a mortality rate of roughly 42 percent among the 10,000 soldiers admitted between 1853 and 1854, according to official medical reports.

Nightingale instituted a system of daily ward inspections, each lasting about 15 minutes, and introduced a standardized chart to record each patient's temperature, pulse and wound condition. The charts, later called the “Nightingale Rose Diagram, ” displayed mortality trends in a visual format; within six weeks the death rate fell to 21 percent, a reduction of half the previous figure as documented by the Army Medical Department.

To improve sanitation, Nightingale ordered the removal of 1,200 pounds of waste from the hospital each day and mandated that all bedding be washed at 140 degrees Fahrenheit, a temperature shown in contemporary manuals to kill cholera bacteria. She also secured a supply of clean water at a rate of 5 gallons per soldier per day, compared with the previous average of 2 gallons, based on a logistical report from the Royal Engineers.

The British government financed the expedition at a cost of £2,000, equivalent to about £250,000 in today’s money, covering transport, supplies and the nurses' salaries. Nightingale reported these expenditures in a letter to the Secretary of War, Sir John Hall, who authorized the budget after the Crimean campaign’s initial casualty count of 25,000 wounded and sick soldiers was confirmed by the War Office.

The success of Nightingale’s methods prompted the British Army to adopt her sanitary guidelines across all overseas hospitals in 1856, as recorded in the Army Sanitary Report of that year. Her statistical evidence influenced the establishment of the Royal Commission on the Health of the Army in 1857, which led to the 1860 Army Medical Act that created a professional medical corps. The reforms reduced the overall military mortality rate by an estimated 15 percent over the next decade, according to a longitudinal study by the British Medical Journal.

Nightingale’s work also laid the foundation for civilian nursing education; the Nightingale Training School opened in 1860 with an initial enrollment of 12 students, and by 1900 it had graduated over 2,000 nurses who staffed hospitals throughout the British Empire. The model of evidence‑based nursing practice introduced in 1854 remains a core principle of modern health care.

Tokugawa Ieyasu wins Battle of Sekigahara, unifies Japan as shogun

21 October 1600 Sekigahara, Japan

Tokugawa Ieyasu secured control of Japan by winning the Battle of Sekigahara. The conflict arose because Ieyasu and the western coalition led by Ishida Mitsunari each claimed legitimacy after the death of Toyotomi Hideyoshi in 1598. Ieyasu commanded the eastern army, estimated at 74,000 men, while the western forces numbered about 82,000. Both sides assembled on the plain of Sekigahara, a narrow valley near the modern city of Fukui, on October 21, 1600.

The eastern army deployed on the western slope of Mount Fuwa, arranging three divisions under the commanders Honda Tadakatsu, Ii Naomasa, and Fukushima Masanori. The western army occupied the eastern slope, placing their strongest units, including the famed Shinsengumi, under the command of Ukita Hideie and Kobayakawa Hideaki. Ieyasu ordered his troops to hold fire until the western forces advanced across the narrow road that linked the two sides.

At 10:00 a.m. the western troops began their assault, pushing the eastern line forward. The decisive moment occurred when Kobayakawa Hideaki, who had been promised a large cash reward, hesitated and then opened fire on his former allies. His troops turned against the western center, creating a gap that allowed eastern forces to pour through. Within two hours the western army collapsed; Ishida Mitsunari fled the field with a small remnant of 5,000 men, while Ieyasu’s forces suffered approximately 5,000 casualties compared with an estimated 30,000 western deaths and captures.

After the battle Ieyasu collected the heads of captured commanders and sent them to the capital, Kyoto, as proof of victory. He entered Kyoto on November 3, 1600, and received the title of shōgun from Emperor Go-Yōzei on March 24, 1603. The shogunate he established, known as the Tokugawa bakufu, centralized political authority, imposed a strict class hierarchy, and enforced a policy of national seclusion that lasted over two centuries. The battle therefore marked the end of the Sengoku period of civil war and the beginning of a unified feudal state under Tokugawa rule.

Portland cement patented, launching modern construction

21 October 1824

Before 1824 most builders used lime mortar, a mixture of limestone, sand and water that set slowly and produced weak joints. Large public works such as the British canals and early railway bridges relied on this material, limiting span length and load capacity. Engineers knew that a stronger, faster‑hardening binder would enable taller walls and longer arches, but no reliable formula existed.

A common myth claims Joseph Aspdin invented Portland cement by accident. Patent records dated 21 October 1824 show that Aspdin, a Leeds mason, deliberately mixed limestone with burnt clay, then heated the blend to about 1,450 °C in a kiln. He described the product as “a kind of artificial stone … which, when mixed with sand and water, hardens into a stone like Portland stone.” The patent specification details the proportions and the firing temperature, confirming a planned process rather than a serendipitous discovery.

The invention mattered because the resulting cement achieved compressive strengths of 3,000 psi, roughly three times that of contemporary lime mortars. Its hydraulic property meant it set under water, allowing construction of foundations in wet conditions. The patent also required that the clinker be ground to a fine powder before mixing, a step that created a uniform paste. Within five years, the Aspdin firm supplied over 1,200 tons of the new cement for projects including the Leeds and Liverpool Canal bridges.

The broader impact unfolded as the material spread to France and the United States. By the 1850s, the United States Geological Survey recorded annual production of 2 million tons, supporting the rapid expansion of railroads and skyscrapers. The ability to cast reinforced concrete columns in the late 19th century traced directly to Aspdin’s formulation, which provided a reliable, repeatable binder for steel reinforcement. Modern construction still uses Portland cement as the base of concrete mixes, with only minor variations in composition.

Aspdin’s patent therefore transformed building practice from a reliance on variable lime mortars to a standardized, high‑strength binder, opening the way for the massive infrastructure and high‑rise architecture that define the modern world.

Seljuk ambush crushes the People's Crusade at Civetot

21 October 1096 Civetot, near Nicaea (modern Turkey)

The People's Crusade, a spontaneous movement of European pilgrims, entered Anatolia in late October 1096 with the intention of reaching Jerusalem. Their leader, Peter the Hermit, a French monk of unknown exact age, organized the march but lacked military discipline. The force assembled at Civetot, a plain near Nicaea, numbered roughly 20,000 men and women, according to the chronicler Albert of Aachen. Most were unarmed peasants; only a small contingent carried swords or spears.

Seljuk Turkish forces in the region were commanded by Sultan Kilij Arslan I, who was about 50 years old and had recently retreated from the earlier encounter at Dorylaeum. He dispatched a detachment of approximately 2,000 mounted archers under the command of his subordinate, the bey Qutb al-Din, to intercept the crusaders. The Seljuks chose an ambush site on a narrow defile near Civetot, where the crusader column would be forced to march in a stretched formation.

On 21 October, Qutb al-Din ordered his horsemen to fire a volley of arrows into the densely packed crowd. The sudden attack broke the Crusade’s loose order; panic spread among the pilgrims, many of whom fled into the surrounding woods. Contemporary accounts record that about 5,000 crusaders were killed in the initial onslaught, while another 10,000 were captured and later sold into slavery. Only a few hundred, including Peter the Hermit, escaped to the coast.

Peter the Hermit survived by reaching the harbor of Smyrna, where he boarded a ship that returned him to Europe. His failure to secure a disciplined force and his reliance on popular enthusiasm are noted by the chronicler Fulcher of Chartres, who wrote that the “people were led by a holy man but lacked the sword of a commander.” The defeat at Civetot halted the People's Crusade, preventing it from joining the later, better organized Princes' Crusade that crossed Anatolia in 1097.

The immediate consequence was the loss of a large number of noncombatants and the demoralization of any remaining supporters in the West. The Seljuk victory also reinforced Kilij Arslan’s confidence in using mobile archery tactics against untrained infantry, a method that would shape subsequent battles of the First Crusade.

President Harding condemns lynching, first presidential anti‑lynching address

21 October 1921 Washington, D.C., USA

The speech that most people associate with President Harding’s stance on racial violence was preceded by a petition from the NAACP and a meeting of the Republican National Committee in early September 1921. Committee members urged the president to address the surge of lynchings that had risen to 64 recorded cases that year, a figure that alarmed many northern legislators but had been ignored by previous administrations.

Harding’s address on October 21 was delivered in the Senate chamber before a joint session of Congress. He explicitly condemned the practice of lynching, calling it a "reprehensible murder" and a violation of the Constitution’s guarantee of due process. The president’s words were not a spontaneous moral outburst; they reflected a strategic decision to appeal to African‑American voters ahead of the 1922 mid‑term elections and to counter criticism that the Republican Party was indifferent to civil‑rights abuses in the South.

The mechanism behind the speech was the use of the president’s bully pulpit, a constitutional authority that allows the chief executive to shape public opinion without needing legislative approval. By speaking directly to both houses of Congress, Harding forced a formal record of federal condemnation, which could later be cited in congressional debates on anti‑lynching legislation. At the time, no federal anti‑lynching bill had passed the Senate because Southern Democrats controlled key committees and repeatedly filibustered proposals.

Harding named specific incidents, such as the murder of a 16‑year‑old boy in Arkansas and the mob killing of a family in Texas, to illustrate the national scope of the problem. He urged the Department of Justice to investigate any federal violations and called on state governors to enforce existing murder statutes. The president’s language was deliberately precise; he avoided calling for a federal law, instead emphasizing the need for states to uphold the rule of law.

The immediate effect was a brief surge in media coverage, with three major newspapers publishing the full text within two days. However, the speech did not translate into legislative success. The Senate rejected the Dyer Anti‑Lynching Bill by a vote of 54 to 44 later that year, and the practice of lynching continued at a rate of roughly one per week until the mid‑1930s. Harding’s condemnation remains the first recorded presidential denunciation of lynching, establishing a precedent that later presidents would expand into concrete civil‑rights initiatives.
