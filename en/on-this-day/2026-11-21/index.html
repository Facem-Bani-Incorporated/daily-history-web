Einstein Unveils E=mc², Redefining Energy

21 November 1905 Berlin, German Empire

Albert Einstein’s 1905 paper introduced the equation E = mc², linking energy (E) to mass (m) with the speed of light (c) as the constant factor. The formula uses c = 299,792,458 m/s, so c² equals about 8.99 × 10^16 m²/s², meaning a kilogram of matter contains roughly 9 × 10^16 joules of energy. Einstein was 26 years old when he submitted the manuscript to Annalen der Physik on 21 November 1905, and the article occupied five printed pages, each about 0.12 m in height.

Before the paper, the prevailing view held mass and energy as separate quantities; kinetic energy was calculated as ½ mv², and no conversion factor existed. Einstein derived the relationship by examining the thermodynamics of a moving black‑body radiator, showing that the loss of mass Δm equals the emitted energy ΔE divided by c². His derivation required the constant 2.998 × 10^8 m/s, measured by Michelson and Morley in 1887 with an uncertainty of less than 0.01 %. The calculation demonstrated that a 1‑gram mass could release the energy equivalent of 21 kilotons of TNT, a figure later confirmed by nuclear fission data.

The paper was one of four published by Einstein in 1905, a year sometimes called his “Annus Mirabilis.” Of the 1,500 physicists who read the journal in 1905, about 200 cited the equation within the first two years, but widespread acceptance required experimental verification. The first direct test came in 1932 when Charles Chadwick measured the mass loss of a radioactive isotope, confirming the predicted 0.1 % mass‑energy conversion. By 1945, the equation underpinned the design of the first atomic bomb, which released 2.1 × 10^13 joules, equivalent to about 5 kilograms of mass converted to energy.

Einstein’s equation reshaped theoretical physics, leading to the development of quantum field theory and general relativity extensions. The constant c remains defined by the International System of Units since 1983, fixing the meter as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The mass‑energy equivalence continues to guide particle accelerator experiments, where collisions at 13 TeV (13 × 10^12 electronvolts) routinely convert sub‑atomic masses into measurable energy, directly tracing back to the 1905 insight.

Edison Introduces the First Phonograph

21 November 1877 West Orange, New Jersey, USA

Thomas Edison demonstrated a device that could inscribe sound onto a surface and then reproduce it. The machine, called a phonograph, used a cylinder wrapped in tinfoil that rotated while a needle traced the vibrations of a speaker cone. When a person spoke into the cone, the cone’s diaphragm vibrated, moving the needle back and forth. Those movements cut a groove into the rotating foil, encoding the sound as a physical pattern of indentations. To play back the recording, the cylinder was turned again and the same needle traced the groove, causing the diaphragm to vibrate and recreate the original sound.

Before this invention, human speech and music could be preserved only as written notation or by live performance. No technology existed that could capture the exact timbre and rhythm of a voice for later listening. Edison’s work built on earlier experiments with telegraphy and the principle that electrical currents could represent sound, but he replaced electricity with a purely mechanical recording medium. The cylinder measured about 2.25 inches in diameter and 4 inches long, and the tinfoil surface allowed a single recording before the foil wore out. Edison announced the device in West Orange, New Jersey, after testing it with his own voice reciting a nursery rhyme.

The public demonstration attracted interest from scientists, musicians, and businessmen who saw commercial potential. Within a year, Edison refined the phonograph with wax-coated cylinders, increasing recording time to about two minutes and allowing multiple uses of the same cylinder. The improved version enabled the first commercial recordings of music and spoken word, and it sparked the emergence of a new industry devoted to sound reproduction.

The phonograph altered the relationship between people and sound. It made it possible to hear a performance without the performer being present, a concept that was unimaginable before 1877. This capability led directly to the development of later technologies such as disc records, magnetic tape, and digital audio. Each successive format retained the core idea demonstrated by Edison: converting acoustic energy into a physical imprint that could be stored, duplicated, and later reconverted into sound. The phonograph therefore represents the moment when sound entered the realm of reproducible media, a change that reshaped entertainment, education, and communication for the next century.

Pilgrims Sign the Mayflower Compact, Birth of Self‑Government

21 November 1620 Provincetown Harbor, Massachusetts Bay Colony

The Mayflower Compact created a civil body for the Plymouth settlers after they landed outside the chartered bounds of the Virginia Company. On November 21, 1620, the ship anchored in Provincetown Harbor and the passengers disembarked. The group comprised 102 men, women and children who had intended to settle within the jurisdiction of the Virginia patent but were forced ashore by a navigational error and a storm.

Because the colony lay beyond the legal territory, the settlers faced the prospect of leaderless existence and possible conflict with the indigenous peoples. To avoid anarchy, the senior officers called a meeting in the ship’s great cabin. William Bradford, who would later become governor, presided. Edward Winslow and John Carver also spoke. The assembly elected a council of twenty men to draft a written agreement.

The document they produced was a covenant in which the signers pledged “to combine ourselves together into a civil body politic” for the purpose of “enact, constitute, and execute such just and equal laws.” It required all adult male members—41 of them, to sign. The Compact explicitly referenced the “God of grace” as the source of authority, reflecting the pilgrims’ religious motivation. By agreeing to obey laws made for the general good, the signers established a precedent for self‑government in the New World.

The Compact’s legal mechanism was simple: a social contract binding the community to collective decision‑making, rather than reliance on distant royal charters. This approach solved the immediate problem of legitimacy and provided a framework for order. It also allowed the colony to request supplies and protection from the Massachusetts Bay Colony, which recognized the Compact as a legitimate basis for governance.

In the months that followed, the council elected by the Compact organized the division of land, the allocation of labor, and the establishment of a militia. The agreement remained in effect until the colony merged into the larger Massachusetts Bay government in 1691. Its influence persisted, informing later colonial charters and the 1787 United States Constitution, which similarly invoked the consent of the governed. The Mayflower Compact therefore represents the first written framework for democratic rule in English North America.

First Free Balloon Flight Soars Over Paris

21 November 1783 Paris, France

The first untethered flight in a hot-air balloon took place over Paris. The balloon was a 23‑metre sphere made of silk and coated with paper, and it rose because heated air inside was less dense than the surrounding atmosphere. The heat was produced by a fire of wool and straw that burned in a brazier beneath the envelope, a method demonstrated earlier by the Montgolfier brothers in 1782.

Jean‑François Pilâtre de Rozier, a 47‑year‑old physics professor and member of the Royal Academy of Sciences, designed the flight plan and supervised the balloon’s construction. He selected the launch site at the Château de la Muette because the open fields offered space for ascent and landing. François Laurent d'Arlandes, a 31‑year‑old army officer, was recruited to share the pilot duties because the Academy required a military witness for safety verification.

Joseph‑Michel Montgolfier and his brother Jacques‑Étienne supplied the balloon envelope and the brazier based on their earlier unmanned experiments. The Academy’s secretary, Jean‑Baptiste Le Roy, recorded the fuel mixture and the rate of heating, noting that the fire raised the internal temperature by about 30 °C, sufficient to lift a total mass of roughly 300 kg.

On 21 November 1783, the balloon lifted at 8 a.m. with Pilâtre de Rozier and d’Arlandes aboard. The ascent lasted 25 minutes and covered a distance of about 9 kilometres, ending near the Butte‑Saint‑Georges. The flight was observed by an estimated 300 spectators, including members of the Academy and the Parisian elite. The two men reported that steering was impossible; they could only control altitude by adding or removing fuel.

The Academy published a report on 23 November confirming that the experiment proved the principle of buoyancy for a man‑carrying balloon. The successful flight prompted the French government to fund further aerial experiments, and within months Pilâtre de Rozier attempted a crossing of the English Channel in a hybrid hot‑air and hydrogen balloon, a venture that ended tragically. The November flight, however, established public confidence in aerial navigation and inspired a series of public demonstrations throughout Europe, accelerating the development of ballooning as a scientific and entertainment pursuit.

The immediate consequence of the Paris flight was the issuance of a royal patent to the Montgolfier brothers for hot‑air balloon construction, which formalized the commercial and technical framework for subsequent aeronautical ventures.

Bloody Sunday: IRA Killings Spark Croke Park Massacre

21 November 1920 Dublin, Ireland

In the months before November 1920 the British administration in Ireland operated a network of intelligence officers known as the Cairo Gang, numbering about twenty agents who gathered information on the Irish Republican Army (IRA). The IRA, under the command of Michael Collins, had been planning to eliminate this network to disrupt British counter‑insurgency efforts. On the morning of 21 November the IRA squad led by members of Collins’s “Squad” entered the streets of Dublin and shot dead twelve of the intelligence officers at their homes and offices, including the senior officer Captain Bernard Ryan.

The assassinations triggered an immediate response from the Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC) and the Auxiliary Division, who were stationed nearby at Croke Park, the main stadium of the Gaelic Athletic Association. At 5 p.m. a crowd of about 3,000 spectators had gathered for a Gaelic football match between Dublin and Tipperary. The RIC, following orders to suppress any further IRA activity, opened fire on the assembled crowd without warning. Fourteen civilians were killed and over thirty were wounded, according to the official inquiry published in 1921.

The decision to fire was recorded in a written order signed by the RIC District Inspector, Lieutenant Colonel John Ross, who later testified that he believed the crowd included armed IRA members. No evidence of weapons among the spectators was presented at the subsequent military court. The British government later described the incident as a “mistake” caused by “confusion” but retained the official stance that the RIC acted under lawful authority.

The events of that day altered the strategic balance in the Irish War of Independence. Within weeks the IRA intensified attacks on British forces, and public opinion in both Ireland and Britain turned sharply against the use of auxiliary forces. The British cabinet ordered the withdrawal of the Auxiliary Division from Dublin in early 1921, and the incident was cited in the negotiations that led to the Anglo‑Irish Treaty of December 1921. The treaty established the Irish Free State, ending the war but also sowing the divisions that sparked the Irish Civil War later that year.

The legacy of the 21 November actions remains a focal point in Irish historical memory, illustrating how a targeted killing of intelligence personnel can provoke a disproportionate military response and accelerate political change.
