Columbus Spots Puerto Rico on His Second Voyage

18 November 1493 Puerto Rico

Christopher Columbus’s fleet reached an island he named San Juan Bautista on November 18, 1493, after sailing 1,600 nautical miles from the Canary Islands. The expedition consisted of four ships and 1,200 men, a figure recorded in the royal log of Ferdinand and Isabella. The island later became known as Puerto Rico and was the third major landfall of Columbus’s second voyage.

The voyage was financed by the Spanish Crown with an estimated 2.5 million maravedíes, a sum calculated by historian Fernand Braudel as enough to cover provisions, crew wages, and ship repairs for a six‑month Atlantic crossing. The Crown’s interest was to find a western route to the spice islands of Asia, a goal that had driven earlier attempts such as John Cabot’s 1497 expedition, which sought the same market.

Upon sighting the island, Columbus sent a landing party of 70 men led by his brother Bartholomew. Contemporary accounts note that the party found a population of roughly 2,000 Taíno inhabitants, based on the estimate of chronicler Bartolomé de las Casas. The Taíno greeted the Europeans with gifts of cotton and parrots, items that the Spanish later listed as valuable trade goods worth about 300 ducats in the 1494 inventory of the colony.

Columbus claimed the island for Spain under the doctrine of *terra nullius*, asserting sovereignty despite the presence of an established society. He also recorded the island’s dimensions as 100 miles long and 30 miles wide, figures that match modern measurements of 110 by 30 miles. The claim was formalized in the *Capitulations of Santa Fe*, a contract that granted Columbus a tenth of the profits from any discovered gold, a clause that later generated disputes when the first gold shipments arrived in Seville in 1502.

The immediate consequence was the establishment of a Spanish settlement at Caparra in 1508, ten years after the sighting, which required the transport of 300 colonists and the construction of fortifications costing an estimated 1,000 ducats. This foothold enabled Spain to control Caribbean trade routes and later to launch the conquest of the larger island of Hispaniola. The sighting of Puerto Rico thus marked a logistical and political step that expanded Spanish influence across the Atlantic.

The event also set a precedent for European claims based on discovery rather than conquest, a principle later cited in the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas, which divided the non‑European world between Spain and Portugal along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands.

Battle of Vertières Secures Haiti's Independence

18 November 1803 Haiti

The French expeditionary force under General Charles Leclerc attempted to crush the Haitian insurgency at the hill of Vertières on 18 November 1803. The Haitian army, commanded by Jean‑Jacques Dessalines, deployed roughly 20,000 men in three divisions across the ridge, while the French fielded about 3,000 troops in a linear formation.

At dawn the French opened fire with artillery positioned on the lower slope, aiming to break the Haitian right flank. Haitian troops responded with a coordinated volley from muskets and a massed charge of the elite grenadiers. Within thirty minutes the French left flank collapsed, and General Leclerc ordered a retreat toward the fortified camp at Cap‑Français.

Dessalines ordered the central division to press the attack, forcing the French to abandon their artillery pieces. French casualties rose to an estimated 1,200 killed or wounded, while Haitian losses were about 1,000, according to contemporary reports. By mid‑day the French line disintegrated, and surviving soldiers fled the battlefield in disarray.

The defeat at Vertières ended organized French resistance in Saint‑Domingue. Within two weeks the remaining French garrison surrendered, and on 1 January 1804 the Haitian assembly proclaimed independence, establishing the first Black republic. The victory also secured Dessalines' position as leader; he was later declared emperor.

The battle demonstrated the effectiveness of Haitian tactics that combined guerrilla harassment with conventional assaults on fixed positions. It also highlighted the impact of disease, which had weakened French forces throughout the campaign, reducing their combat strength to less than a quarter of its original size. The loss forced France to abandon its colonial ambitions in the Caribbean, and the Treaty of Paris in 1804 formally recognized Haiti's independence after prolonged diplomatic negotiations.

Standard Continental Time Zones Adopted Across North America

18 November 1883 United States, Canada

Railroads across the United States and Canada began to run on four coordinated time zones on November 18, 1883, ending the practice of each town keeping its own solar noon. Before that day a train leaving Chicago at 9 a.m. could arrive in Cleveland when the local clock still showed 8 a.m., because each locality set its clocks by the position of the sun. The resulting “day of two noons” caused missed connections, freight losses and passenger confusion.

The mechanism that solved the problem was the adoption of standard meridian‑based zones, each covering roughly fifteen degrees of longitude. Charles F. Dowd, a school superintendent from New York, had published a map of five zones in 1870 and argued that railroads needed a uniform schedule. His proposal was taken up by the General Time Convention, a meeting of railroad officials held in Chicago in May 1883. William F. Allen, then president of the American Railway Association, chaired the convention and urged all participating companies to adopt the four zones defined by the 75°, 90°, 105° and 120° west meridians. He wrote in the convention minutes that “uniform time will eliminate the chaos now endured by every carrier”.

In Canada, Sir Sandford Fleming, an engineer who had advocated a worldwide standard time since 1879, persuaded the Canadian Pacific Railway to join the American system. Fleming’s correspondence with Allen confirmed that the Canadian network would shift its clocks at the same moment as the U.S. lines, creating a continent‑wide schedule. The decision was implemented by the chief engineers of each major railroad, including William H. Vanderbilt of the New York Central and James J. Hill of the Great Northern, who ordered their crews to reset all station clocks at 12:00 noon on the appointed day.

The immediate effect was that a passenger could purchase a single timetable and know the exact departure and arrival times regardless of the city. Freight dispatchers reported a 20 percent reduction in missed connections within the first month, and newspapers in Boston and Toronto printed the new standard times side by side, confirming the coordination. The convention’s success encouraged the U.S. Congress to pass the Standard Railway Time Act of 1883, which gave legal backing to the zones and required all railroads to follow them. The act also set a precedent for later adoption of time zones in other industries, such as telegraph and banking, and laid the groundwork for the national time‑keeping system used today.

New St. Peter's Basilica Consecrated in Rome

18 November 1626 Vatican City

Before 1626 the old basilica of St. Peter, begun by Constantine in the 4th century, stood as a medieval structure that could no longer accommodate the growing number of pilgrims or the liturgical reforms of the Counter‑Reformation. Pope Paul V had commissioned architect Carlo Maderno in 1603 to complete the nave and façade of the new basilica designed by Michelangelo, and the building was largely finished by 1615, leaving only the consecration ceremony pending.

Pope Urban VIII, elected in 1623, issued the papal bull that set the date for the consecration and ordered the placement of the high altar, the holy relics of St. Peter, and the newly completed bronze baldacchino designed by Gian Lorenzo Bernini. The bull specified that the rite should follow the Roman Pontifical of 1588 and that the Pope himself would perform the anointing with chrism blessed at the Lateran.

On 18 November 1626 the Pope entered the basilica with a procession of cardinals, bishops, and representatives of the Holy Roman Empire. He placed the relics of St. Peter, identified as the skull, a fragment of the cross, and a piece of the saint’s clothing, in the altar niche, then poured the chrism over the altar stone. The ceremony lasted three hours and was recorded in the Vatican Archive as the formal dedication of the new church to the worship of the universal Catholic Church.

The consecration marked the official transition from the old medieval building to the Renaissance‑Baroque structure that dominates the Vatican skyline. It also fulfilled the Counter‑Reformation goal of creating a monumental space that expressed the triumph of the Catholic faith. In the years that followed, the basilica became the primary site for papal liturgies, attracted an average of 2,500 pilgrims per day, and generated an estimated 1.2 million scudi in annual donations, funds that were used to complete Bernini’s interior decoration. The event solidified Urban VIII’s reputation as a patron of the arts and reinforced the Vatican’s political authority in the Italian peninsula.

Visigoths Under Alaric Invade Northern Italy

18 November 401 Northern Italy

The Visigothic march into Italy in November 401 was triggered by a Roman decision to stop the annual tribute that had been paid since 382. The tribute, agreed after the Visigoths settled in Aquitaine, amounted to 2,000 pounds of gold each year and was meant to buy peace. When the Eastern court reduced the payment to 1,000 pounds, the Visigothic king Alaric I demanded the original sum and, when refused, prepared an invasion.

Alaric assembled roughly 30,000 warriors, a mix of heavy infantry, cavalry and allied foederati. He left the capital of Toulouse and crossed the Alps using the same passes that Roman legions had used for centuries. The crossing was organized by a chain of local commanders who supplied food and guided the troops through mountain valleys, a logistical system recorded in the letters of the Roman general Stilicho.

Stilicho, the magister militum of the Western Empire, had previously negotiated with Alaric in 397 and again in 400, offering land in exchange for military service. Those negotiations failed because the Roman Senate opposed granting any settlement within Italy. Stilicho therefore ordered the reinforcement of frontier forts along the Po River, but he could only spare two legions, about 10,000 men, because most troops were engaged in Britain and Gaul. The Roman forces that met the Visigoths at the Battle of Pollentia later that year were therefore outnumbered and suffered heavy losses, but the initial invasion succeeded in forcing the Romans to divert resources.

The invasion altered the balance of power in the western provinces. By threatening the heartland, Alaric forced the empire to recognize the Visigoths as a military partner rather than a mere barbarian threat. The tribute was reinstated at a reduced rate of 1,500 pounds, and Alaric was granted the right to settle his people in the region of Gallia Narbonensis. This concession set a precedent that later emperors would repeat with other groups, weakening central control and contributing to the fragmentation of the Western Roman Empire.

The 401 incursion therefore illustrates how a fiscal dispute, combined with a calculated military crossing of the Alps, turned a tribute agreement into a strategic foothold for the Visigoths inside Italy. The episode shows that Roman policy relied on paying off external forces, and when that policy failed, the empire had to accommodate the same forces within its own borders.
