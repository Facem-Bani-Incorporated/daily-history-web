Portugal Conquers Ceuta, Launching Age of Discovery

21 August 1415 Ceuta

Twenty-one-year-old Prince Henry, a boy barely out of his breeches, found himself not at the helm of a grand caravela charting unknown seas, but scrambling over the blood-slicked stones of Ceuta. He was not yet ‘the Navigator’ – that moniker would arrive decades later, a retrospective award for a life dedicated to distant horizons. On August 21, 1415, he was simply Henry, the third son of King John I of Portugal, desperate for a purpose beyond courtly intrigue, eager for the clatter of steel and the roar of a captured city. This was his first taste of battle, and he approached it with a ferocity that surprised even his seasoned father.

The target was Ceuta, a jewel of a port on the North African coast, a hub for the gold, spices, and slaves flowing from the Sahara. For the Marinid Sultanate, it was a vital artery; for the Portuguese, a tempting prize. King John I had spun a tale of a holy crusade, a noble endeavor to strike a blow against Islam, but the whispers among the 20,000 men gathered in the Lisbon harbor spoke more of loot and glory. The king’s sons – Duarte, Peter, and Henry – had lobbied hard for the expedition, their youthful ambition a powerful lever against their father’s more cautious temperament. They craved knighthood, earned not by decree, but by conquest.

The deception was masterful. The vast Portuguese fleet, over 200 ships, sailed first towards Gibraltar, feigning an attack there. The Marinid governor of Ceuta, Salah ben Salah, a man long accustomed to the ebb and flow of Mediterranean trade, watched from his battlements, relieved. He believed the Portuguese were after Gibraltar, a more obvious target. He relaxed his guard, a decision that would cost him dearly. When the armada, under cover of a moonless night, swerved back towards Ceuta, he was caught utterly unprepared.

As dawn broke, casting long, nervous shadows across the harbor, the Portuguese landed. Henry, a whirlwind of youthful zeal, was among the first ashore. He led a charge so reckless, so headlong into the city’s defenses, that he was quickly separated from his bodyguards. Wounded early, he fought on, a testament to pure, unadulterated adrenaline. His older brothers, Duarte and Peter, were not far behind, demonstrating similar courage, their hunger for martial renown palpable in every swing of their swords.

The fight for Ceuta was brutal. The Marinid defenders, once roused, fought fiercely from houses and alleyways, but their initial disorganization proved fatal. The Portuguese, well-armed and disciplined, pushed through the narrow streets. The city, normally a bustling marketplace of cultures, became a labyrinth of screams and steel. By afternoon, it was over. The Portuguese flag flew over the Grand Mosque, which was swiftly consecrated as a Catholic church. The city was plundered, its wealth redistributed among the conquerors. Salah ben Salah, stripped of his authority, watched his city burn.

Ceuta was a triumph, and Henry, along with his brothers, was knighted on the field of battle. Yet, the victory was a hollow one in many respects. The city, once a bustling trade hub, withered under Portuguese rule. Merchants, fearing piracy and the disruption of traditional routes, simply rerouted their goods to other North African ports. Ceuta became an isolated, expensive outpost, a financial drain rather than a golden goose. Portugal had gained a strategic toehold, but the promised wealth evaporated, forcing Henry to look elsewhere – to the uncharted waters and distant shores that would, eventually, earn him his famous, if anachronistic, title. The boy who fought for a city would spend the rest of his life dreaming of what lay beyond the horizon, precisely because his first major conquest yielded so little of what he truly sought.

Pueblo Revolt Captures Santa Fe, Expelling Spanish

21 August 1680 Santa Fe

The water, it seems, always runs out eventually. For Governor Antonio de Otermín and the last few hundred Spanish colonists huddled inside Santa Fe's Casas Reales, it has been a week since the ditches dried up, since the Pueblo warriors, numbering in the thousands, severed their supply. Seven days of thirst, seven days of siege, seven days of watching the fires of rebellion burn hotter around the plaza. The air, thick with dust and desperation, crackles with the unspoken question: *how much longer?*

It is August 21, 1680. The sun beats down on the adobes, a relentless witness. Inside, Otermín knows time is a luxury he can no longer afford. He has seen the bodies of his people, dragged from the fields, mutilated, a stark message from the unified pueblos. He has seen his capital, a bastion of European supremacy for seventy years, reduced to a desperate, thirsty island in a sea of Indigenous fury.

Now, a decision. Otermín looks at the faces of his remaining soldiers, some 100 fighting men, along with women and children. Thirst is a cruel master, but the alternative, surrender, feels like a death sentence of a different sort. So, in a move born of sheer desperation, he orders a sortie. A last, audacious gamble.

At this precise moment, the heavy wooden gates of the Casas Reales groan open. A small, ragged column of Spanish soldiers, their armor glinting dully in the morning light, pushes out into the plaza. It is a forlorn hope, a final, desperate charge against an overwhelming force. The Pueblo warriors, who have been maintaining a watchful, patient siege, are momentarily caught off guard. They had anticipated the Spanish would simply wither. This sudden, violent lunge is unexpected.

The Spanish, fueled by the primal urge to survive and perhaps a touch of suicidal courage, fight with a ferocity born of their predicament. They hack and thrust, aiming to scatter the Pueblo lines, to carve a path, any path, to escape. For a breathless hour, the plaza becomes a maelstrom of close-quarters combat. The sound of steel on hide shields, the shouts, the cries, fill the air. It is a brief, brutal, bloody interlude, a final, futile gasp of colonial dominion.

But the numbers are simply too vast. The Pueblo warriors, organized and determined, absorb the Spanish thrust. They are not retreating; they are simply reforming, encircling. Otermín's gamble fails. His men are driven back, broken, into the confines of the palace. The gates clang shut once more, sealing their fate. The Spanish have lost 47 men in this last, desperate push. They have gained nothing but a deeper understanding of their own vulnerability.

The Pueblos, led by the enigmatic Popé, had orchestrated this uprising with chilling precision. Messengers had carried knotted cords, each knot representing a day until the revolt, across hundreds of miles and dozens of distinct pueblos, speaking different languages but united by a shared grievance. The Spanish discovered the plan only days before, when two Tiwa leaders were captured and tortured, but it was already too late. Popé simply advanced the date. The irony, of course, is that the Spanish had spent decades attempting to eradicate the Pueblo's own complex, sacred forms of communication and social structure, only to be undone by a remarkably effective indigenous network.

That night, with no water left and their numbers dwindling, the Spanish begin their ignominious retreat. They slip out under the cover of darkness, leaving Santa Fe to the Pueblos. The colonists, once masters, are now refugees, trudging south towards El Paso del Norte. It will be twelve long years before they even attempt to return, and when they do, they will find a city cleansed of their influence, its kivas rebuilt, its sacred fires rekindled. The land, it seems, remembered who truly belonged.
