Isabella I of Castile Dies, End of an Era

26 November 1504 Castile, Spain

Isabella of Castile died on November 26, 1504 in Madrigal de las Altas Torres. She had been suffering from a prolonged fever that began in early October and worsened despite the physicians’ attempts with bloodletting and herbal remedies. On the morning of the 26th she received the last sacraments from the archbishop of Toledo, then slipped into unconsciousness at about nine o’clock and passed away at noon.

Her death ended a reign that had begun in 1474 and that had united Castile and Aragon through her marriage to Ferdinand of Aragon. The union created the Spanish Crown, a political structure that pooled the revenues of both kingdoms while keeping their separate laws. Isabella’s policies had completed the Reconquista with the conquest of Granada in 1492, and she had authorized the expedition of Christopher Columbus that same year, establishing a transatlantic empire. By the time of her death, Spain controlled territories in Italy, the Americas and the Atlantic, and the crown’s treasury held an estimated 30 million maravedíes in annual revenue.

The succession was predetermined by the 1479 Treaty of Granada, which stipulated that the crown would pass to the eldest surviving child of the monarchs. Isabella’s only surviving child, Joanna, known as Joanna the Mad, was the legal heir. However, Joanna’s husband, Philip the Handsome, claimed the title of king of Castile and demanded joint rule. Ferdinand, as co‑monarch of Aragon, invoked the 1500 Act of Union that gave him regency over Castile during the queen’s incapacity, and he moved to the Castilian court in Valladolid to secure the transition.

On November 28, two days after Isabella’s death, the Cortes of Castile convened to confirm the succession. They recognized Joanna as queen but appointed Ferdinand as regent until Joanna could produce an heir. Philip was allowed to enter the court but was barred from exercising sovereign authority. This arrangement preserved the joint monarchy while preventing a split of the empire between the Habsburgs and the Spanish crown.

In the months that followed, Ferdinand’s regency stabilized the administration, collected the taxes needed for the war in Italy, and oversaw the final phases of the Inquisition’s expansion. The balance of power in Europe shifted as the Habsburg dynasty, through Philip, began to influence Spanish affairs, setting the stage for the eventual inheritance of the Spanish throne by Charles V in 1516. Isabella’s death therefore marked the end of a personal rule but also triggered the legal mechanisms that kept the Spanish kingdoms united under a single crown.

Vlad the Impaler Triumphs Over Basarab, Secures Wallachian Throne

26 November 1476 Wallachia

Vlad III reclaimed the Wallachian throne after defeating Basarab Laiotă with the help of Stephen of Moldavia. Contemporary Moldavian chronicles state that Stephen contributed roughly 12,000 cavalry while Vlad mustered about 8,000 infantry and light horsemen. Basarab’s force, according to the 16th‑century historian Grigore Ureche, numbered close to 30,000 and included mercenaries from the Ottoman borderlands.

The decisive mechanism was a coordinated pincer movement on the plain near the village of Râmnicu. Stephen’s cavalry approached from the north, forcing Basarab’s right wing to tilt inward. Simultaneously Vlad ordered a rapid advance of his infantry across the central ridge, striking the enemy’s centre. The plan relied on timing; Stephen’s messengers delivered the signal at dawn on 26 November, and Vlad’s troops began their charge within ten minutes. Basarab’s commanders, recorded as Ioan and Radu, failed to redeploy their reserves, leaving the centre exposed.

Vlad directed the infantry personally, positioning his son, Mircea, at the front of the left flank to inspire the troops. Stephen placed his son, Bogdan, on the leading cavalry squad to ensure obedience. Basarab’s right wing suffered the heaviest losses, with contemporary accounts noting that of the 12,000 men engaged there, only about 2,500 survived. Overall casualties are estimated at 15,000 for Basarab’s army and 3,000 for Vlad and Stephen combined.

The victory secured Vlad’s third reign over Wallachia, lasting until his death in 1476. Stephen’s involvement was motivated by a mutual interest in curbing Ottoman influence, a motive confirmed by a letter from Stephen to the Hungarian king dated early 1476. The win also forced Basarab Laiotă into exile in Transylvania, where he sought refuge with local nobles. Vlad’s restored rule allowed him to re‑impose his harsh justice system, which historians link to a temporary reduction in banditry recorded in the Wallachian tax registers of 1477.

In the months following the battle, Stephen and Vlad negotiated a treaty that defined the border along the Carpathian ridge and stipulated joint defense against future Ottoman incursions. This agreement held until Stephen’s death in 1504, shaping the political landscape of the region for the next generation.

John Harvard’s Bequest Launches America’s First College

26 November 1607 Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA

John Harvard’s birth in Southwark, England, set the financial conditions that later enabled the first college in the British colonies. His father, Robert Harvard, was a prosperous brewer who left a sizable estate when he died in 1621. The elder Harvard’s wealth allowed his son to attend Emmanuel College, Cambridge, where he earned a Bachelor of Arts in 1628 and a Master of Arts in 1631. After completing his studies, John Harvard migrated to the Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1637, where he became a minister at the newly formed congregation in Charlestown.

The mechanism that matters is the bequest made in John Harvard’s will. He died of tuberculosis on September 14, 1638, at the age of thirty‑one. His will directed that his library of 400 books and a cash donation of £780 be given to the “college” that the colony was planning to establish. The colony’s leaders, including Governor John Winthrop, used the cash to purchase land and to fund the construction of the first building. The book collection became the core of the college library, and the cash covered roughly one‑third of the initial building costs, which totalled about £2,300.

Popular narratives often claim that John Harvard founded the college, but contemporary records show that the institution was a joint venture of the colonial legislature and the local clergy. The bequest was a decisive contribution, yet it was one of several sources of funding, including a later grant of £400 from the English Crown in 1650. The college’s charter, granted in 1650, lists the colony’s General Court and the ministerial body as the governing entities, with no single individual credited as founder.

The consequence of the bequest was immediate. By 1639 the college began classes in a modest timber building, and the library grew to over 700 volumes within a decade, attracting students from across New England. The institution, renamed Harvard College in 1639 to honor the donor, survived the English Civil War and later expanded into a university. The original £780, equivalent to roughly £120,000 in today’s money, illustrates how a single private donation could shape colonial education and produce an enduring legacy of higher learning.

Charles IV Crowned King of Germany, Consolidating Imperial Power

26 November 1346 Bonn, Holy Roman Empire

Charles IV was elected King of the Romans in 1346 after the death of Louis IV, but his authority remained contested by rival claimants and by the Imperial cities that had resisted his predecessor. The election gave him the legal right to rule, yet he lacked the ceremonial affirmation that secured recognition across the empire.

On 26 November 1346 Charles travelled to Bonn, a city that had supported his election, and received the imperial crown from the Archbishop of Cologne. The coronation followed the traditional rite prescribed by the Golden Bull of 1356, which required the presence of three electors and the anointing with holy oil. By completing the ceremony, Charles transformed his claim from a contested election to a formally recognized kingship, allowing him to issue imperial edicts with undisputed legitimacy.

The immediate effect was the consolidation of his control over the Imperial Diet, which had previously been divided between supporters of Louis IV and those of the papal-backed anti-king. With the crown in his hands, Charles could summon the Diet in 1347 and begin negotiations that led to the Golden Bull, a constitutional document that defined the electoral process and limited the power of the emperor. The coronation also secured the loyalty of the Rhineland princes, who provided troops and taxes that funded his later campaigns in Italy.

Before the coronation, the empire faced a dual power structure: the elected king lacked full ceremonial authority, and the major cities retained considerable autonomy. After the ceremony, the empire operated under a single recognized monarch whose legal authority was reinforced by the established coronation ritual. This shift reduced the influence of rival claimants and gave the emperor a clearer basis for exercising imperial rights, such as appointing bishops and adjudicating disputes among the princes.

Charles used his newly affirmed position to pursue a policy of centralization. He negotiated the 1355 Treaty of Brünn, which confirmed the election of his son Wenceslaus as king, thereby establishing a hereditary succession that had been absent since the 13th century. The coronation in Bonn therefore marked the moment when Charles moved from a contested ruler to a universally acknowledged king, setting the stage for the legal reforms that defined the Holy Roman Empire for the next two centuries.

Song Navy Wins Decisive Battle at Caishi, Halting Jin Advance

26 November 1161 Yangtze River, China

The Song navy defeated a Jin invasion fleet on the Yangtze River using ship‑mounted trebuchets that launched gunpowder bombs. Emperor Gaozong ordered the defense after the Jin court demanded tribute, and he appointed the veteran commander Li Yue to lead the operation. Li Yue assembled about 800 warships carrying roughly 12,000 soldiers and equipped each vessel with several trebuchets capable of hurling explosive projectiles.

The Jin army dispatched an armada of roughly 1,200 ships with an estimated 30,000 troops, intending to cross the river and force Song forces into open battle. Jin commanders planned to rely on numerical superiority and the speed of their shallow‑draft vessels. Song intelligence reported the Jin approach, allowing Li Yue to position his fleet at the narrowest point near Caishi, where the current slowed the attackers.

At the moment of engagement, Song trebuchets launched gunpowder‑filled shells that exploded on impact, igniting wooden decks and causing panic among the Jin crews. Contemporary records state that of the 1,200 Jin ships, about 600 were destroyed or abandoned within the first two hours. Jin casualties are reported as 10,000 dead or wounded, while Song losses numbered fewer than 1,000, primarily from boarding actions that the Song repelled with crossbows and short swords.

The victory secured the Yangtze as a defensive barrier and forced the Jin to abandon further large‑scale river assaults for the remainder of the campaign season. Song control of the river allowed the central government to maintain supply lines to southern provinces and to continue tax collection without interruption. The battle also demonstrated the practical military value of gunpowder projectiles, prompting the Song court to increase production of fire bombs for both naval and land forces. This development accelerated the diffusion of gunpowder warfare across East Asia in the following decades.
