Battle of Gettysburg Concludes—Union Victory Secured

3 July 1863 Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, USA

On the morning of July 3, 1863, a twenty-four-year-old Virginian named George Pickett sat on his horse and watched 12,000 men prepare to walk straight into the bloodiest twenty minutes in American military history.

The first two days at Gettysburg had been grinding and indecisive. The Union Army of the Potomac, led by George Meade—a competent man with the temperament of a provoked wasp—had claimed the high ground. The Confederate Army of Northern Virginia, led by Robert E. Lee, was 140 miles from home, running low on ammunition, and running out of options. Lee decided on something that even his most trusted general, James Longstreet, thought suicidal: send nearly a third of his remaining men across open ground in full daylight to crack the Union center.

At one o'clock in the afternoon, about 150 Confederate guns exploded. The earth trembled. Union soldiers flattened themselves into the dirt. The Union artillery answered—75 guns, all aimed at the same stretch of ridgeline. For two hours it was pure mechanical thunder. Men covered their ears though it made no difference. Then, silence.

Pickett turned to Longstreet. "Sir, I have my orders." He rode toward his division.

At 1:20 p.m., they stood. The infantry rose from the ground, straightened their lines, dressed their uniforms. About 12,000 men—though some say 15,000; nobody was counting in what came next. They could see the Union position half a mile across an open field, grass knee-high, stone walls ahead. No cover. No woods. No cavalry to flank. Just 2,400 yards of open Pennsylvania ground between themselves and 6,000 riflemen.

They walked. Contemporary accounts say they moved like troops on parade—flags unfurled, officers on horseback, neat ranks. Union soldiers watched and something strange happened: they felt admiration, even pity. "We couldn't help but admire the gallantry," a Union officer wrote. Then they opened fire.

The first volley dropped men in rows. The ones behind stepped over the bodies and kept walking. The cannons switched to canister—cans of ball bearings exploding outward like shotguns. Men's chests simply ceased to exist. Officers screamed orders that nobody could hear. The survivors walked faster. Some actually reached the wall—a handful even vaulted it. One Confederate soldier planted his regimental flag atop the Union parapet. A Union soldier killed him. By 1:40 p.m., it was finished.

Of the men Pickett sent forward, about 9,000 were killed, captured, or wounded. Fewer than 1,000 made it back unscathed. Lee watched from the ridge, his face unreadable. When the survivors staggered back, he rode among them. "It was all my fault," he told the hollow-eyed men. "It was my fault."

Pickett never forgave him. Decades later, an interviewer asked what he did during the Civil War. Pickett paused and said: "I was there, sir. I was there when the Union committed murder." He meant the canister. He meant his own men. But he never mentioned Gettysburg again—as if naming it aloud might let the ghosts follow him north.

George Eastman, Pioneer of Roll Film, Born

3 July 1844 Waterville, New York, USA

A photographer in 1880 carried 50 pounds of equipment to take a single picture. The camera itself cost $200—more than a year's wages for a factory worker. The glass plates were fragile, the chemical baths temperamental, and the process required the patience of a monk and the precision of a surgeon. You didn't take photographs; you performed them.

On July 3, 1844, in Waterville, New York, a boy was born who would make all of this absurd.

George Eastman didn't invent photography. But he invented something far more useful: he made it ordinary. Before Eastman, a camera was a tool for professionals and the obsessively wealthy. By the time he was 44 years old, any person with 25 dollars could own one. The mental shift was enormous. Photography went from being something you studied for years to something you did without thinking.

The 1888 Kodak camera was a wooden box about the size of a small loaf of bread, weighing just over a pound. You looked through a tiny window, pressed a button, and trusted it. No adjustments. No expertise required. The company loaded it with 100 feet of roll film before it left the factory—enough for 100 exposures. When you'd finished, you mailed the entire camera back to Eastman's company in Rochester, New York, paid 10 dollars, and waited two weeks. They developed the pictures, reloaded the camera with fresh film, and sent everything back to you.

The genius was not in the camera itself. It was in the system. Before Eastman, you bought a camera and you were responsible for everything: the chemicals, the darkroom, the ruined plates, the failures. You became a chemist by necessity. With Kodak, the company took that 90 percent of work you didn't want to do and made it vanish. You bought simplicity in a box.

Eastman's real innovation wasn't roll film, though he did invent that—a flexible strip of cellulose that could hold image after image without shattering. His real innovation was understanding that most people didn't want to be photographers. They wanted to take pictures. There's a world of difference, and it's worth 25 dollars to avoid the other one.

Within 15 years, Kodak controlled roughly 80 percent of the American camera market and 50 percent of the world market. A company founded by a boy born on a July morning in upstate New York had made photography a democratic art. You didn't need a calling or a fortune. You just needed 25 dollars and curiosity.

Here's the thing nobody mentions: Eastman himself was a shy man who rarely spoke in public. He built an empire on understanding what other people wanted, not on charisma or luck. He died in 1932 having given away 75 million dollars—more than half his fortune—to charity and education. In his suicide note, he wrote simply: "My work is done." A man who made the world see itself had finally stopped looking.
