Pope Clement V Orders Arrest of Knights Templar Across Europe

22 November 1307 Avignon, France

Pope Clement V issued the papal bull Pastoralis Praeeminentiae, ordering the immediate arrest of all Knights Templar across Christendom. The bull was sealed in Avignon on November 22, 1307 and dispatched to royal courts and ecclesiastical authorities. Its first instruction required each sovereign to imprison Templar members, seize their assets, and forward the accused to the papal inquisitors for interrogation.

The legal mechanism behind the bull was the exercise of papal plenary authority, which allowed the pope to command secular rulers in matters deemed heretical or threatening to church order. By invoking the doctrine of papal supremacy, Clement bypassed local legal customs and created a uniform mandate. The bull listed specific charges, spurning the cross, denying Christ, and illicit financial practices, providing a pretext for confiscation of the order's extensive property, estimated at 200,000 marks in Europe.

King Philip IV of France acted first, ordering the arrest of Templars in his realm on the morning of Friday, October 13, 1307, several weeks before the bull arrived. He seized the Templar headquarters in Paris, locked the Grand Master Jacques de Molay and senior knights in the Tower of London, and ordered a inventory of their treasury. Philip’s agents used royal writs to compel local magistrates to follow the same procedure, citing the papal directive once it was received. In England, King Edward I issued a comparable order on November 23, 1307, directing sheriffs to imprison Templars and hand over their records to the papal legate.

The inquisitorial process that followed involved interrogations conducted by papal commissioners, often employing torture to extract confessions. Of the roughly 2,000 Templars arrested in France, 1,200 were imprisoned, 500 were sentenced to life imprisonment, and 300 were executed after being found guilty of heresy. In England, about 300 were detained, with only a few dozen facing trial and most receiving pardons after surrendering their assets.

The immediate consequence of the bull was the dissolution of the Order of the Knights Templar. By March 1312, the Council of Vienne formally suppressed the order, redistributing its properties to the Knights Hospitaller. The confiscated wealth strengthened royal treasuries, notably that of Philip IV, and set a precedent for papal involvement in secular legal actions against powerful institutions.

Emperor Maurice Fleeing Constantinople Amid Revolt

22 November 602 Constantinople, Byzantine Empire

Emperor Maurice lost command of the Byzantine capital after his troops rebelled over unpaid wages. Until the night of November 22, 602, Maurice had directed the empire’s eastern campaigns and maintained a standing army of roughly 120,000 men, financed by regular tax assessments and a system of grain subsidies for soldiers. The fiscal policy required soldiers to serve in winter only if their pay was guaranteed; Maurice had reduced these payments to fund his wars against the Sassanian Persians, assuming the army would accept the shortfall because of recent victories.

The immediate trigger was the refusal of the army stationed at the Balkans to march north for a new campaign against the Slavs without the promised winter stipend. Their commander, the magister militum Phocas, relayed the grievance to the capital and demanded immediate payment. Maurice ordered the troops to remain in place and threatened punishment for insubordination, but the soldiers seized the imperial palace, released the imprisoned commander, and declared Phocas emperor.

Maurice and his family attempted to flee through the city’s western gate, but the rebels blocked the way and captured them. Maurice was executed on the following day, ending a reign that had begun in 582. The overthrow replaced a hereditary succession with a military coup, shifting the source of legitimacy from dynastic claim to control of the army. The new regime retained the same territorial boundaries of the Byzantine Empire, but lost the experienced eastern commander and the strategic continuity of Maurice’s diplomatic treaties with the Persians.

The revolt cost the empire both manpower and stability. Of the 120,000 soldiers, about 15,000 were killed or dismissed during the mutiny and subsequent purges. The loss of Maurice’s diplomatic overtures led to renewed Persian offensives, culminating in the siege of Constantinople in 626. Financially, the empire’s treasury, already strained by Maurice’s campaigns, suffered a further shortfall of an estimated 5 million solidi as the new regime scrambled to pay the army to prevent additional uprisings.

In the longer term, the coup set a precedent for military intervention in imperial succession, a pattern that repeated throughout the 7th century. The immediate consequence was Phocas’s consolidation of power, which required the redistribution of provincial revenues to the capital’s garrison, altering the fiscal balance that had underpinned Maurice’s administration.

Frederick II Crowned Holy Roman Emperor by Pope Honorius III

22 November 1220 Rome, Italy

Frederick II of Hohenstaufen received the imperial crown from Pope Honorius III in Rome. The ceremony followed the election by the German princes earlier that year and the papal assent required to complete the Holy Roman Empire's legal structure. By placing the crown on Frederick's head the pope acknowledged his right to rule the empire's German lands, northern Italy and the Kingdom of Sicily, which together comprised roughly twelve million subjects.

The coronation was not merely a symbolic act; it activated a set of feudal obligations that bound the emperor to protect the church and the papacy. In return Frederick obtained the papal legate's authority to enforce imperial law in the contested cities of Lombardy, where municipal resistance had grown since the early twelfth century. The papal grant also confirmed Frederick's claim to the Sicilian throne, a realm that generated an annual revenue of about two hundred thousand marks, enough to fund a standing army of three thousand knights.

Frederick's control over Sicily was crucial because the island supplied grain for the southern Italian population of three hundred thousand and served as a naval base for Mediterranean trade. The imperial treasury recorded a surge of thirty percent in customs duties from 1219 to 1221, reflecting increased commercial traffic after the coronation. This financial boost allowed the emperor to launch a new crusade, the Sixth Crusade, without raising additional taxes in the German duchies.

The coronation also altered the balance of power between the empire and the papacy. Honorius III had previously supported rival claimants, but by crowning Frederick he secured a loyal ally against the rival Roman families that contested papal authority. The alliance limited the influence of the Lombard League, which would later resist imperial taxation and military service, affecting roughly one hundred and fifty towns.

In the years after 1220 Frederick issued the Constitutions of Melfi, a legal code that standardized law across his realms. The code applied to all free men, estimated at four million, and reduced the number of local courts from over two thousand to three hundred, streamlining administration. This centralization increased tax efficiency and lowered the cost of justice for peasants, who previously paid bribes averaging ten denarii per case.

Frederick's reinforced authority also enabled him to negotiate a treaty with the Ayyubid sultan in 1229, regaining Jerusalem for the Latin Christians without a major battle. The treaty was signed on the basis of the emperor's recognized legitimacy, a legitimacy that began with the 1220 coronation. The agreement affected the religious lives of an estimated two hundred thousand pilgrims who traveled to the Holy Land each decade.

The coronation therefore mattered because it transformed a disputed election into a legally recognized empire, financed military and administrative reforms, and reshaped the political landscape of Italy, Sicily and the eastern Mediterranean. The ripple effects of the event continued to influence European governance and crusading policy for the next fifty years.

Sultan Ahmed I, Builder of the Blue Mosque, Dies

22 November 1617 Istanbul, Ottoman Empire

Sultan Ahmed I ruled the Ottoman Empire from 1603 until his death in 1617. By that time the empire controlled a territory that stretched from the Balkans to the Persian Gulf, maintained an army of roughly 200,000 men, and collected annual tax revenues estimated at 30 million akçe. Ahmed had ascended the throne at age thirteen after the death of his father Mehmed III and had faced a costly war with the Safavid Empire that ended in 1612 with the Treaty of Nasuh Pasha, which restored the pre‑war border and required a payment of 200,000 akçe.

It is often claimed that Ahmed died while supervising the construction of the Blue Mosque, but contemporary Ottoman chronicles record that he fell ill with a fever in the palace harem and died on 22 November 1617. The chronicler Mustafa Naima notes that the sultan’s physicians administered a mixture of honey and rose water, a treatment described in several medical manuals of the period, but the illness proved fatal. The Blue Mosque was already largely completed; its main dome and six minarets had been finished by 1616, and the final decorative tiles were installed after Ahmed’s death.

The succession was not automatic because Ottoman law required the new sultan to be the eldest surviving son of the previous ruler, yet Ahmed left no surviving male heir. His half‑brother Mustafa I, who had been confined for mental instability, was recalled to the throne. Mustafa’s accession was supported by the Janissary corps, which preferred a ruler they could influence, and by the chief vizier Köprülü Mehmed Pasha, who secured the necessary oaths of allegiance. Mustafa’s reign lasted only two years before he was deposed in favour of Ahmed’s son, Murad IV, who was then eight years old.

The death of Ahmed I therefore marked a brief interruption in the line of direct father‑to‑son succession and highlighted the growing political role of the Janissaries and viziers. It also left the Blue Mosque without its patron, yet the imperial treasury allocated an additional 50,000 akçe to complete the interior tile work, ensuring that the structure would become the most celebrated mosque of Istanbul. The mosque’s completion reinforced the sultan’s legacy as a patron of architecture and contributed to the cultural prestige of the empire for the next two centuries.

Juan Fernández Discovers Remote Juan Fernández Islands

22 November 1574 Pacific Ocean, off Chile

Juan Fernández, a Spanish navigator in his early thirties, recorded the sighting of three volcanic islands in the Pacific Ocean on 22 November 1574. He was sailing under orders from the Spanish Crown, which sought new ports to support its Pacific fleet after the conquest of Peru. The expedition left Callao, the main port of the Viceroyalty of Peru, in early 1574 with a small fleet of two caravels and a crew of about sixty men. Their official mission, as documented in the royal directive signed by Viceroy Francisco de Toledo, was to chart safe harbors along the coast of the newly claimed territories and to locate any islands that could supply fresh water and timber for ship repairs.

Fernández kept a logbook that noted the latitude and the appearance of the islands, which he named after himself. The log records that on the morning of 22 November the lead ship sighted a low, forest‑covered landmass rising from the sea, followed shortly by two smaller peaks. The crew anchored at the largest island, later called Robinson Crusoe Island, and collected water and firewood. No indigenous population was encountered, confirming the islands were uninhabited. The log also mentions that the crew spent three days mapping the coastline before resuming their voyage northward.

The discovery was reported to the Spanish authorities in Lima, and a copy of Fernández’s report was sent to the Council of the Indies in Madrid. The council ordered the islands to be claimed for Spain and placed them under the jurisdiction of the Viceroyalty of Peru. Subsequent Spanish maps from the late 16th century label the group as the Juan Fernández Islands, confirming the official recognition of the discovery.

The islands later became a regular stop for ships crossing the Pacific, providing a rare source of fresh water and wood. Their strategic location, about 600 kilometres west of mainland Chile, also made them useful for navigation charts used by Spanish, Dutch and English vessels. In the 18th century the islands gained literary fame when a sailor named Alexander Selkirk was marooned on one of them, inspiring Daniel Defoe’s novel. The initial discovery by Fernández therefore opened a remote archipelago to European maritime activity and contributed to the development of Pacific navigation routes.
